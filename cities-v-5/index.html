Where does Boston stand?

Boston is a city in Massachusetts. Each of its measures is set here against every other US place, and beside the places most like it.

Your city is not an island

People live in one town and work in another, and firms hire from all over the region. The economy that matters is that wider labor market, read here at the scale of the : for Boston, the Boston MSA. Much of what follows is read at that scale.

Not everyone who lives in Boston works there

55% of the jobs held by Boston’s residents are in Boston itself (54% in 2014). The other 157,434 are elsewhere, mostly in Cambridge, Newton, Waltham and Quincy. The map shades each place by how much of its workforce lives in Boston, up to 26% in Brookline.

More people commute into Boston than out of it

Of the 718,571 jobs in Boston, residents hold 26%. The other 528,488 are filled by commuters, mostly from Quincy, Cambridge, Somerville, Brookline and Newton. Winthrop sends 41% of its working residents here.

That makes 207 jobs here for every 100 its residents hold. Follow these exchanges outward and you trace, roughly, the edges of the Boston MSA.

    Your City’s Most Specialized Industries

    Of the tradable industries above, these are the ones Boston does an unusual amount of. Each is measured against the national benchmark: a score of 1.0 means the industry takes up the same share of jobs here as it does across the country. Anything higher means the city is more concentrated in it than the country is — and the further past 1.0, the more distinctive it is.

    The same measure, now set against four cities of similar size and economic make-up. The tick on each row is the average of those four — where Boston’s mark runs past it, the city is more specialized in that industry than its peers; where it falls short, less. Hover any row to see the cities behind the average.

    The same measure, now read against every US metro at once — the teal dot is where the Boston metro lands. Hover any mark for the city and figures behind it.

    Take the share of your city’s jobs that sits in an industry, then divide it by the share of jobs that same industry holds nationally. Economists call the result revealed comparative advantage, or RCA.

    If an industry is 4% of jobs in your city and 1% of jobs nationally, the score is 4.0 — four times more concentrated here than in the country at large. A score of 1.0 means exactly average, and below 1.0 means the industry is thinner here than it is elsewhere.

    A high score doesn’t automatically mean a big industry — it means a distinctive one. Employment is carried in the row tooltip, and in the dot form by the size of each dot.

    Switch to Compare with peers above to hold the same scores against four metros in a similar position.

    In this view each peer is scored exactly the same way against the same national benchmark, and the tick on every row is the average of those four. Comparing against the country at large mostly tells you that Boston is a large, wealthy city; comparing against metros in the same position tells you something you can act on.

    Glossary

    The terms this tool uses, and how each is measured.

    Complexity

    Complexity measures the knowledge an industry takes to run. Work that needs many people holding many different specialized skills, such as medical devices, financial engineering and research instruments, is complex. Work that almost anywhere can do, such as food service, retail and basic construction, is not.

    It matters because complex work is hard to copy. Places that hold it tend to pay more, to weather downturns better, and to find it easier to move into the next complex thing, because the know-how is already in the room. A metro’s complexity is the mix of its industries weighted by how much of each it does.

    Tradability

    Each industry is placed in a tier by how much of what it makes can be sold to someone outside the metro.

    Traded
    sells most of its output outside the metro, so its earnings are new income arriving here
    Partly traded
    sells some outside and some to the people already here
    Local
    serves the people already here; the customer has to be standing there

    The tiers are cuts on a continuous measure, so an industry near a cut could fall the other way.

    Specialization

    An industry counts as a specialization when the metro does more of it than the country does. The measure divides the industry’s share of jobs here by its share of jobs nationally: 1.0× is exactly the national rate, 2.0× is twice the national share.

    It says nothing about size. A small industry can be a powerful specialization and a large one can be entirely ordinary. This measures what is distinctive about the metro, not what employs the most people.

    Metro area

    A metropolitan statistical area (MSA) is how the US Census draws the functional city: a dense core and every county tied to it by commuting. It is the closest official shape to a labor market, the area within which people can change jobs or homes without changing lives.

    The Boston MSA, Boston-Cambridge-Newton, has 130 places and 5,025,517 people (2024). Boston itself holds 13.4% of them. That is why most of what follows is read at the metro scale.

    Peer metros

    The reference build compares Boston with five peer metros: Washington, Seattle, San Diego, Denver and Baltimore. The figure for each industry is the metro’s share of its jobs measured against the five peers’ share taken together, instead of against the nation’s: 1.0× is the same share as the peers, 4.5× four and a half times theirs.

    The ranking’s black mark is the peers’ own rate against the nation, which follows from the two: where it falls short of the metro’s bar, the metro is the more specialized of the six. The “Against peers” order ranks the industries by that lead, the metro’s rate less the peers’, and draws it as the bar from 0: to the right where the metro is ahead, to the left where it is behind.
