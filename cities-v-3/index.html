Your city is not an island

The administrative city is one piece of a larger machine: people commute, firms hire, and housing supply responds across a region far wider than the city line — the labor market. We approximate it with the metropolitan statistical area. Boston sits inside its metro area, and most of what follows is read at that scale.

A metropolitan statistical area is how the US Census draws the functional city: a dense core plus every surrounding county tied to it by commuting. It is the closest official shape to the real labor market — the area within which people can change jobs or homes without changing lives.

Boston’s metro spans 197 cities and towns and about 4.9M people; the administrative city is one of them, home to roughly 13% of the metro’s residents. That is why most of what follows is read at the metro scale.

Not everyone who lives in Boston works there

Boston’s residents hold 334,026 jobs, and 54% of them are inside the city itself (55% in 2015). The other 154,904 are somewhere else — above all Cambridge, Newton, Waltham and Quincy.

Boston is a net importer of workers

Read the other way round: 687,736 jobs sit inside Boston, and 26% of them are held by its own residents. The remaining 508,614 are filled by people commuting in, mostly from Quincy, Cambridge, Somerville, Brookline and Newton.

Your City’s Most Specialized Industries

Of the tradable industries above, these are the ones Boston does an unusual amount of. Each is measured against the global benchmark: a score of 1.0 means the industry takes up the same share of jobs here as it does worldwide. Anything higher means the city is more concentrated in it than the world is — and the further past 1.0, the more distinctive it is.

The same measure, now set against four cities of similar size and economic make-up. The tick on each row is the average of those four — where Boston’s mark runs past it, the city is more specialized in that industry than its peers; where it falls short, less. Hover any row to see the cities behind the average.

The same measure, now read against every US metro at once — the teal dot is where the Boston metro lands. Hover any mark for the city and figures behind it.

Take the share of your city’s jobs that sits in an industry, then divide it by the share of jobs that same industry holds worldwide. Economists call the result revealed comparative advantage, or RCA.

If an industry is 4% of jobs in your city and 1% of jobs globally, the score is 4.0 — four times more concentrated here than in the world at large. A score of 1.0 means exactly average, and below 1.0 means the industry is thinner here than it is elsewhere.

A high score doesn’t automatically mean a big industry — it means a distinctive one. Employment is carried in the row tooltip, and in the dot form by the size of each dot.

Switch to Compare with peers above to hold the same scores against four metros in a similar position.

In this view each peer is scored exactly the same way against the same global benchmark, and the tick on every row is the average of those four. Comparing against the world at large mostly tells you that Boston is a large, wealthy city; comparing against metros in the same position tells you something you can act on.
