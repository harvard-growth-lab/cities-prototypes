## **Introduction** 

We usually think about economic growth at the country level, but most economic activity actually happens in cities. The 9 billion people in the world do not live evenly spread out. People tend to concentrate in space.  In the U.S., urban economic activity accounted for 90% of total GDP in 2023, and 80% since the early 2000s (US Bureau of Economic Analysis). In the 1940s, only 25% of the global population lived in cities. Today, over 50% of the world's population lives in cities, and by some estimates this number is expected to increase to 70% by 2050 ( _World Urbanization Prospects 2025: Summary of Result_ , 2025). 

Living in cities, of course, comes with costs. These include costs of congestion, pollution, transportation, and competition for space and amenities. However, the fact that cities are population magnets indicates that the benefits of living in cities outweigh these costs. 

We live in a world of cities because being close together allows us to combine our skills in productive ways. Industries require a wide range of productive capabilities to function. These capabilities can be codified in machines or manuals, but many of them reside in people, their skills, experience, and tacit knowledge. Thus, economic growth is the process of accumulating knowledge across specialized workers and firms to produce more and more complex products and services (Hidalgo and Hausmann, 2009). 

Cities work as spaces of agglomeration, where people come together to recombine their know-how in productive ways. In cities, workers can connect with others who possess complementary know-how, and firms can find the right mix of talent, suppliers, and services to operate efficiently. As industries become more complex, requiring a wider range of inputs, they become increasingly dependent on the interconnected productive ecosystems that cities provide. This is why larger cities tend to be more complex, and why larger, more complex cities pay higher wages (i.e., the urban wage premium). 

Cities are not only places where knowledge is concentrated; they also enable the creation of new knowledge. Population density in cities increases the likelihood of encounters across fields, disciplines, and perspectives. These interactions generate new combinations of ideas and practices, which serve as the raw material for innovation. Research in urban economics has long shown that innovation, as measured by patents or R&D activity, is disproportionately concentrated in cities. However, innovation extends beyond patents; it also encompasses the creation of new occupations, the emergence of new firms, and the ongoing redefinition of what cities do. Cities like San Francisco, Shenzhen, or Bangalore have not always been centers of technology, but have evolved into such through an iterative process of experimentation, investment, and know-how accumulation and recombination. 

The dynamics of economic growth in cities differ from those observed at the national level. Most countries think of themselves as small open economies, meaning that the world is much bigger than themselves and thus they can assume that their actions do not 

5 

significantly affect what happens at the global level. For them, interactions with the world through exports, imports, and financial flows are very important.  In particular, they take global prices and interest rates as given, unaffected by what they do. We can think of a city as an even smaller, and more open economy.  They are smaller than the country they are in. They can export and import not just to the rest of the world, but also to the rest of the country. In fact, if exports are important to small open economies, they are even more important to cities, meaning by exports the things that are done in the city and sold outside of it, not necessarily abroad. 

However, indicators of success at the national level, such as rising income per capita, are not as useful in a city context, and may even be misleading. This is because unlike across small-open economy countries, people are free to move in and out of the cities within a country more freely. When people and firms can move freely, they do so when the benefits of moving outweigh the costs. Their decisions depend on the combination of things that a city has to offer, including housing and transportation costs, job opportunities, security, educational quality, healthcare, quality of life, weather, and other factors. The fact that people have options means that they will move until there is what is called spatial equilibrium: the overall welfare that the marginal individual can achieve in one place is no better than in another. If jobs in city A pay more than in city B, there will be some so-called compensating variation to make the person not want to move. This includes such things as more expensive housing, more congestion, different weather, and so on. This spatial equilibrium exists within countries but not between countries, because people usually are not as free to move permanently to other countries. 

Thus, labor supply is much more elastic at the city level than at the national level, because outsiders can move in when good things happen while residents can leave, in response to bad news. This greater elasticity means that good and bad shocks can show more in total employment and population changes than in wages and incomes per capita. When wages do go up, it may be an indication that firms are forced to pay their workers more because the city is becoming less attractive. Alternatively, if it is becoming more attractive, it may be an indication that the city is particularly bad at providing the extra housing, transportation, schools, hospitals, and security services that will be demanded by the growing population, causing an increase in the cost of living, congestion and crime. However, figuring out which story is correct for each city is not always straightforward and requires carefully piecing together and triangulating many pieces of data. 

These odd dynamics in the economic growth of cities made us decide to develop a growth diagnostic framework that is fit for purpose. We need to adapt the growth diagnostic framework to the urban setting because the urban setting changes the interpretation of the symptoms. Additionally, theories of cities developed by the growing field of urban economics can be highly illuminating about how cities work and why they have the shapes and features that they have, but they need to be adapted in order to use them as diagnostic 

6 

tools[1] . This report aims to fill that gap with a framework that can be implemented by researchers in both urban planning and economics, as well as policymakers and practitioners working on cities. This framework is a synthesis of the knowledge gained at the Growth Lab in diagnosing economic growth problems in cities, states, and provinces.[2] 

The basic approach to growth diagnostics starts from the assumption that production requires many inputs, including millions of different tools and materials but also a plethora of specific skills, infrastructure services such as energy, water, internet connectivity, waste disposal, logistics, transportation, security and support services, as well as rules, and regulations. In general, these inputs are complements, not substitutes. Substitutes are things like coffee and tea. Complements are things like coffee and sugar. If you do not have coffee, you might substitute it with tea. But if you do not have sugar, you may not want to drink the coffee you do have. 

Clearly, you cannot replace a tractor with a car, or lack of electricity with more security. These things complement each other, and just like with coffee and sugar, complements are best consumed in some proportion. One implication of this complementarity is that you run out of some inputs before you run out of others. You might have neither much coffee nor much sugar, but given your preferences, you probably will run out of one before the other. If you run out of coffee, you would be desperate for more and be willing to pay quite a bit for it, but you will not want to pay much for sugar if you do not have coffee. 

Two implications emerge from this context. First, if production requires many complementary inputs, it will be limited by inputs that are in relatively short supply. We call these the _binding constraints_ . Second, the inputs that are in shortest relative supply will have high prices, as society would be willing to pay a lot to get more of them. But their scarcity will cause the price of their complements to be low. This gives us a diagnostic strategy into figuring out what may be constraining growth. Rather than addressing long lists of universal best practices or evaluating micro interventions using operationally expensive empirical methods, growth diagnostics focuses on identifying the constraints that, if addressed, would provide the highest bang for the buck. We flip the question from "What are the best-practice policies that the international experience has shown are effective in addressing growth issues?”  to "What factor is constraining growth in this particular context, what are its underlying causes and what actions might relieve the constraint?"

The framework we develop in this report aims to help researchers and practitioners diagnose growth problems in an urban setting. The report is organized as follows: 

**Section I** presents the basic dynamics of growth in cities. We summarize the main findings of the urban economics literature and connect it to the ideas of economic complexity that puts knowledge at the center of production and emphasizes that knowledge grows through the division of knowledge – putting different bits of knowledge in different heads. 

But using knowledge in production involves bringing different heads back together again. Cities are the places where these heads are brought together for production. Here the emphasis is on collective know-how, capabilities, diversification and exports, appropriately redefined and measured at the city level. 

We then move on to explain the concept of spatial equilibrium and its implications. We reflect on the difference between cities and countries: most countries are small open economies, strongly influenced by interactions with the outside world but not able to move global variables, such as prices. Lastly, **Section III** expands the analytical tools and methods for finding and interpreting diagnostic signals in the data. We illustrate them through a set of brief case studies that demonstrate the framework in practice.

## **Section 1: Thinking About Cities** 

## **Summary** 

How do cities work? What does success for a city look like? In what ways might a city struggle? Do we need to think about cities differently than the way we think about countries? 

All of these questions are important for understanding how to build a diagnostic framework for cities. Thankfully, the answers to many of these questions can be drawn from work across different disciplines ranging from economics, urban planning, economic complexity, and history. Drawing on these disciplines can help us understand what a city needs to achieve to function well. And when we understand this, we can then diagnose the challenges when a city struggles. 

Drawing on these diverse fields, this section presents five key insights about how cities function which form the basis of our later diagnostic framework. Specifically, this section will show that: 

1. **Exports are a city’s oxygen:** No city is self-sufficient. A city’s survival depends on its ability to import the things its residents need but do not produce. But to pay for these imports, the city will ultimately need to trade for them: it will need to export, meaning that it will need to find things that can be produced in the city and sold to nonresidents. But these non-residents are free to buy from others. Therefore, these exports need to be not just good enough, they need to be competitive. The size and growth of the city will ultimately depend on the size and growth of its (appropriately redefined) export sector. 

2. **Cities grow by accumulating know-how and using it in diversifying their production towards more complex goods and services. But the growth of knowhow and the pattern of diversification of cities show strong path dependencies.** Know-how is limited at the individual level, but it can grow at the social level by putting different bits of know-how in different heads. The division of knowledge goes hand in hand with the growth of knowledge. Production requires knowledge that does not fit in a single head. Firms are where the different heads come together to make things. The higher the complexity of a product, the larger the network of people with different skills that must come together. The power of cities is their ability to put specialized workers and firms in close enough proximity to be able to meet. As the diversity of the city’s know-how grows, more industries and more complex industries become feasible. It is easier for a new industry to emerge if it can leverage the knowhow and capabilities that the city already has and maybe add a few missing ones through immigration and investment. Also, agents in the city or newcomers can discover new ways in which existing know-how and capabilities can be recombined. 

9 

3. **Cities and their physical form facilitate or hinder matching workers with very diverse know-how with firms that have very diverse labor needs.** The ease with which workers and firms can be matched is key to a city’s success. For this to happen, workers need good and affordable housing, security, access to education, and other amenities and must be able to commute with ease to their places of work. Firms will need to find places where they can locate and have access to the necessary infrastructural services and attract a broad swath of potential workers. The physical shape of the city, the location of the spaces assigned to residential, industrial and commercial use, and the transport infrastructure connecting them will determine how well firms and residents can participate in the economic activity of the city **.** If it takes hours for workers to commute to their jobs, or housing is unaffordable given the wages that industries can afford, then the city will not be able to function. This makes the evolved physical form of a city crucial, and that physical form is the result of the interaction between firm and household location decisions, market forces, and urban regulation. 

4. **Cities are de facto competing with other cities for workers and firms, which has important implications for a city’s growth possibilities.** Workers are free to move in and out of the city, generating a dynamic that stops when spatial equilibrium is achieved. At that point, workers are indifferent between living in the city or elsewhere. At that point, the welfare of the marginal worker is equalized across locations: higher wages are not necessarily a reflection of good things such as higher productivity, but of bad things such as higher housing costs, crime, or worse education and other amenities. Good news in the rest of the country may translate into outmigration from the city until higher wages compensate for the city’s other disadvantages. This means that what happens in one city will have an impact on all cities. For cities to attract workers and firms they must consider these forces. 

5. **Growth in one city can lead to gains for the rest of the region or country:** When one city- call it A - is able to grow by expanding its export sectors, it can draw in more people and firms from the rest of the country (and abroad). It will also want to buy from those places, creating a positive demand shock for them. The combination of a negative shock to their labor supply and a positive shock to the demand for their exports, will lead the rest of the country experience higher wages. This means that growth in one city can lead to higher incomes across the whole country, implying that cities can be engines of prosperity and economic development for the country as a whole. However, for this to happen, city A should be able to elastically accommodate the rise in its population. If not, the positive shock to city A may just translate into higher housing costs and other disamenities meaning that the potential multipliers associated with the growth of city A will be muted. 

10 

Each of these observations are interesting by themselves. However, taken together, they paint a picture of what successful city growth looks like. In particular, a successful city will be one where: 

- There are thriving, growing export sectors that fund imports and non-tradable jobs. 

- These sectors are built on accumulated diverse, specialized know-how in workers and firms. 

- The city allows all residents to benefit from the city’s labor market and amenities at reasonable costs. 

- People find the benefits and costs of the city are attractive enough to live there relative to other cities. 

- And where growth in the city spills over into growth for the country as a whole. 

A city will be unable to sustain jobs (in both tradeable and non-tradeable sectors), if its export sector has problems. Its export sectors risk stagnation if it is unable to accumulate know-how by attracting specialized workers and firms. These firms and workers will be unable to work together effectively if the city is too expensive or too congested. The city will lose the people and firms it needs to sustain itself if it becomes less attractive or falls behind other cities. Finally, if cities cannot accompany the growth of its export industries by accommodating a higher population, then the entire nation will be worse off. The insights in this section will provide the basis for a city growth diagnostic framework in Section II.

## **1.1 Exports are a City’s Oxygen** 

## **Cities and Specialization** 

Take a look at any city in the world, and you will see a breathtaking array of different economic activities. You will see people going into office buildings day in and day out to manage retirement savings by transacting on financial markets halfway around the world. You will see individuals serving or eating in restaurants and bars or people going to a play or a concert. You will see people getting treatment in a specialized hospital or architecture firms drawing up plans for new buildings. You might see artisans and artists making their next masterpiece or dockworkers unloading goods from every part of the planet. 

It is because individuals in a city specialize in such specific jobs and occupations that the city is able to have so many different activities going on. And it is because of all these activities that the city can attract people to want to live there. To have arts, finance, and medical industries in the same city, you need specialized artists, fund managers, and doctors in that city. Specialization also allows workers to join together to produce things that they never could on their own. It is because the hospital has workers specialized as doctors and nurses in many different specialties, but also accountants, administrators, and custodial staff, that the hospital as a whole can perform complex services like surgeries or long-term care beyond what an individual doctor can do on their own. 

Since Adam Smith’s Wealth of Nations, published in 1776, we have understood that the division of labor and specialization are key to prosperity. Adam Smith also remarked on how there is a deep relationship between cities and specialization. Chapter 3 of that book is aptly titled _The Division of Labor is Limited by the Extent of the Market._ Smith describes how in small towns and rural areas, “every farmer must be butcher, baker, and brewer, for his own family”, and how “There are some sorts of industry, even of the lowest kind, which can be carried on nowhere but in a great town” (Smith, 1776). 

As Smith explains, one of the challenges of specialization is that you can only support that level of specific work if there is enough demand for it. In a village of a few households, there will not be enough business for the butcher, baker, or brewer to make a living solely on their niche, specialized efforts. However, in a big city, the density and proximity of so many people helps bring together many potential customers. The butcher, brewer, and baker can focus on their specific work without having to worry about there not being enough people wanting to buy the fruits of their labor. 

Still, there is another challenge with specialization. If you specialize, then you do not produce everything that you consume. If you are a specialized insurance broker, you cannot survive directly by consuming the insurance sales you make. The insurance broker will want to buy groceries, pay for a place to live, buy the latest computer or phone, go see a doctor for medical care, or go to the movie theatre. All of these are things that they will need to buy 

12 

from others. The benefit of living in a city is that the insurance broker can focus on insurance, earn money, and then buy all of these things from others in a city. 

However, not everything that people in a city consume will be produced there. The groceries the insurance broker bought were likely grown outside the city. The computer or phone he buys was likely made many miles away. Throughout history, there have been things that can be made in cities, but other things that are only possible or better produced outside of them. For example, today’s cities generally do not grow their own food. Density and proximity are excellent for coordinating the work of many complex activities, but food production requires much more land per person. Similarly, many other industries are ones that are better placed outside of dense cities where land is less scarce and expensive (see Box 1) and where there is less of the need to coordinate a diverse and specialized workforce. 

This means that cities cannot be (and should not aspire to be) self-sufficient. They must rely on the production of goods and services outside their borders made by non-residents. A city’s ability to bring in these other goods and services limits how large the city can be. Take the case of the very first cities which emerged after the development of sedentary agriculture. Agriculture allowed for surpluses which meant people could specialize away from food production to other specialized roles (e.g., being warriors, priests, merchants, blacksmiths). These specialized workers would want to live in proximity to each other. Making a chariot means being in proximity to the metal worker, the wheelmaker, the horse trainer, and noble who would use the chariot. These places would be the first cities. 

However, these cities still needed to feed their population by bringing in food from their hinterlands. In a very real sense, the population of the city was directly limited by how much food the city could bring in from outside its boundaries. Without enough food, it does not matter if there are many people who are willing to buy candles from the candlemaker in the city. The candlemaker and their customers will starve. 

In other words, the city needed to be able to import food to sustain itself. This is true not just for food, but for all other things that its population needs but which its residents do not produce. The wood worker and baker will need wood and flour, the metal worker raw materials, etc. To get these “imports”, ancient cities needed to either extract them through tribute, taxes, and conquest or trade for them by selling things that non-residents wanted. 

Cities today still need to be able to import to survive. They continue to need food and raw materials, cars, computers, cell phones, antibiotics, X-ray machines, electricity, internet and many other things. The more specialized a city’s residents become, the more that they will need to rely on others outside the city. Just like in the cases of ancient cities, a city’s ability to pay for its imports directly limits its potential size. It may not be a case of starvation, but if a city cannot support imports, then people will leave, and the city will die.

## **Box 1. What is Produced in and Outside a City? Fermions vs Bosons** 

In physics, there are two types of fundamental particles. Fermions are particles like electrons and protons while bosons are particles like photons, gluons and gravitons. The major difference between these two is that fermions cannot occupy the same (quantum) space at the same time. They do not like being around other particles. On the other hand, bosons can occupy the same (quantum) space at the same time. 

Similarly, there are industries and activities that do not play nice with others and so will prefer to be in places where there are fewer other things going on. By contrast, there are other industries that like being in close proximity to other industries. Two forces determine whether an industry will play well with others and be like a boson or repel other industries and be like a fermion. 

First, if an industry uses lots of land, or has very large emissions and effluents, then it will tend to want to be on its own and be more like a fermion. Second, if the industry uses lots of complementary, specialized knowledge, skills, and occupations it will benefit from density and proximity to other industries. Thus, bosonic industries will be those that use less land and more specialized, complementary knowledge. Fermionic industries will use more land and less of that specialized, complementary knowledge. Bosonic industries are more likely to want to locate in larger cities. Fermionic industries will want to locate in smaller, more rural places.

## **The Importance of Tradeable Income and Exports** 

The question is, then, how do modern cities support the imports that its residents need? The answer is very similar to that for a country. In the economics literature, we have the idea of a small, open economy. It is small in the sense that it has to take the world’s prices as given, and it is open in the sense of being open to trade, investment, and the movement of people. Cities are like small open economies; except they are even smaller and even more open to all of these forces. 

Small open economies must be able to finance imports, and their ability to do so is captured in their Balance of Payments (or BoP). The BoP of a country captures all of the ways that it generates income from people outside of the country on the credit side and all the things it pays foreigners for on the debit side.  Exports, foreign aid, remittances or investment income will show up on the credit side and imports, debt service or investment outflows will show up on the debit side. 

Like a country, a city also has a BoP. This BoP captures all the income generated from nonresidents on the credit side and all of the ways residents of the city pay non-residents on the debit side. Like in the case of a country or any other type of “double-entry book-keeping”, the credits must balance out with the debits. This accounting makes it abundantly clear: to pay non-residents for things a city wants (the debit side), the city must generate some sort of external income (the credit side). 

How can cities generate income from non-residents to pay for imports? Some cities, especially capital cities, might be able to tax the rest of the country or the proceeds from natural resources that are extracted elsewhere. As studied by Gollin et al., these cities will be “consumption cities” with many jobs, occupations, and industries in non-tradeable sectors that meet local demand. Residents can afford to pay for imports from outside the city (and country) by taxing the rest of the country (Gollin, Jedwab and Vollrath, 2016).  A capital city in a mineral-rich country like Abu Dhabi, Lima, Riyadh or Santiago might fit this category. 

Another way cities could finance imports is if there is a large investment boom in the city. Money from the country’s or world’s financial system might flow into investments in real estate and shopping malls. This income can be used to import and will help the city population to grow. The booms in many cities in the US prior to 2008 were fueled by a boom in capital inflows that financed real estate bubbles (Mian, Straub and Sufi, 2020). The money flowing in not only raised property prices but fueled population growth and jobs in nontradables. 

Alternatively, a city might get inflows of government grants or of remittances from relatives who live elsewhere. Central governments usually collect taxes nationally and make transfers to different state and city governments, often in ways that favor the less developed 

15 

parts of the country. Similarly, in very poor, aid-dependent countries, cities might be sustained by large inflows of foreign donor aid, or by foreign remittances. 

But the dominant way in which cities finance their imports is through exports: they buy the things produced elsewhere by trading for them, offering goods, services, and experiences to non-residents. However, in the case of cities, we need to take a more expansive view of what is exportable. By exports, we simply mean anything that a non-resident buys from a city. So, this certainly includes the usual suspects of goods and services that appear in BoP statistics. But it can also include many things that would normally be considered nontradeable at a country level. 

A city with an excellent third-tier hospital will find many non-resident patients who have come to be treated. For countries where health services are paid by national programs, hospitals can be treated as exports, even if they treat locals, because payment is coming from outside of the city. Colleges and universities attract out-of-town students, who bring money into the city. A city might have the only airport in its region and therefore nonresidents must come to the city to fly. The same might be said about major sports teams that have a regional fan base. While we generally think of tourism as something like an export, for cities this definition becomes even more expanded. A city with an amazing restaurant or theatre district might be a magnet for non-residents to come into the city to spend both on the attraction and other goods and services in the city. The export activities of cities are often their defining characteristic. Los Angeles and Mumbai are known in part for their movie industries that are exported to the country and the world. New York, Johannesburg, London, and Hong Kong are known as regional and international financial centers. The San Francisco Bay Area is defined by the tech industry it hosts. Understanding what a city exports is critical to understand what sustains a city and often determines its dynamics. The steel and auto industries were central for the success of the Rust Belt cities of the American Mid-West, and the decline of those industries starting in the 1980s has everything to do with the problems those cities have had to face. 

Exporting industries create direct jobs in their respective industries. For example, many in Silicon Valley are employed in the tech industry and many in New York work in finance. But the money earned from exports and spent locally supports many other jobs through what is known as the local multiplier. People in a city need to buy groceries, hire plumbers, send their kids to school, see doctors etc. The income from exports can then support the income of all of these ancillary goods and services that local residents demand. In a classic paper, economist Enrico Moretti calculated the local multiplier in the United States. He found that every job in manufacturing at the city level generated 1.6 additional jobs in non-tradeable industries, and every high-skilled tradeable sector job generated 2.5 jobs in non-tradeable industries (Moretti, 2010). 

16 

In this way, a boom in exports causes a broad-based rise in city population and income, well beyond the direct employment in the export industry. Historically, most cities start around an export activity, which creates the initial justification for the development of the nontradable goods and services to support city life. 

By contrast, a bust in a city’s export sector can be devastating. As the export sector shrinks, so too does the city’s capacity to support both non-tradeable jobs and imports. Just as a diminished ability to import food would crater the populations of ancient cities, diminished import capacity can cause a large exodus from the modern city.

## **1.2 Cities Diversify by Accumulating Know-How** 

In the previous subsection, we discussed the importance of exports to a city’s growth. Here we zoom into the process of growth and diversification of exports. In most successful cities, the composition of exports evolves as the city grows, with new industries joining the mix. The stories of how new industries appear in cities are usually fascinating. And they are often important parts of a city’s history. We will show the pattern of how cities succeed or fail to diversify their export base is a rich interaction between path dependence and serendipity. While the story in each city is different, we can understand the mechanisms of diversification and exports in cities by applying the know-how lens given by the theory of Economic Complexity and what we commonly call the _Scrabbly Theory of Development._ 

## **The Scrabble Theory of Development** 

The neoclassical growth theory, developed by Robert Solow (Solow, 1956) (Solow, 1957) highlighted the importance of technological progress in sustaining growth, but did not shed light on its source. So-called endogenous economic growth theory, starting with Romer (1990), Lucas (1988), Grossman and Helpman (1993) and Aghion and Howitt (1992) puts the emphasis on the incentives to innovate and grow knowledge. The Economic Complexity approach starts from a similar vantage point, by putting knowledge at the center of the story. After all, to make anything, you need to know how to make it. 

But how do you access or acquire knowledge? Where does this knowledge reside? Suppose you want to prepare a recipe from a cookbook. The recipe talks about the ingredients you need, like a piece of meat, tomatoes, onions, salt, etc. It also details the tools you need, such as a skillet, an oven or a blender.  It says nothing about how to grow tomatoes or onions, how to raise and slaughter cows or how to make skillets and ovens. The knowledge of how to make those tools and materials is already embodied in them. You need not know how to make them to use them. However, you need other people to know how to make those things for you to prepare the meal. 

The recipe also presumes some knowledge from its readers. First, it is written in a certain language, so you need to have acquired knowledge of that language before you can use the 

19 

cookbook. Second, it presumes knowledge of cooking, so it can put into words processes that are hard to describe to a person that has not done it before. For example, it may say that you should cut the onions in small cubes and fry them in olive oil until they caramelize. If you have never cut and fried onions before you may not know how to cut them or when they have caramelized. 

In short, the knowledge that you use to cook is in part in the cookbook, in part in the ingredients and tools you use and in part in your brain. This example shows that knowledge takes three forms: it is embodied in the tools and materials that others have produced and that we can use. It is codified into codes, recipes, formulas, algorithms and how-to-domanuals such as cookbooks. But it must also rely on some knowledge that resides in brains and only in brains. 

Tools and intermediate inputs save on the knowledge we need to have in our heads because you do not need to understand all the science and engineering that went into making them to use them. In a way cars, computers and skillets are the physical embodiment of the knowledge that went into making them. It is knowledge that others have but that we do not need to have in order to access its benefits. 

The same can be said about codified knowledge. If we have developed a new dish or a new surgical procedure, we might share the insights by writing it up. And we can access the knowledge that has been codified by others. 

But you can have all the tools of a dentist and all the books on dental surgery, but that does not mean you will know how to perform a dental operation. This third type of knowledge exists in human brains and only in brains. We call this type of knowledge ‘tacit knowledge’ or “know-how”. 

Now, the economy has been exhibiting exponential growth, but our individual capacity to know has not: we are not individually more knowledgeable than the great sages of the past. How can knowledge grow exponentially at the societal level with a limited amount of knowledge at the individual level? After all, despite the growth of knowledge, a college degree still lasts 4 years and a master’s degree is stuck at 2 years. How can knowledge grow exponentially at the societal level, given the limited ability of humans to master it? 

The gist of the answer goes back to Adam Smith: the division of labor allows for a division of knowledge: we put different bits of knowledge in different heads so that the whole can know more than its parts. 

But this creates a challenge for the accumulation and use of knowhow. Since we spread knowhow across different brains, the use of knowhow in production requires bringing together a team of brains that span the needs of the production process. Different products will require different combinations of know-how or capabilities and hence different teams. 

20 

Here, a metaphor may help fix ideas. We can think of the different bits on knowhow that we put in different brains as letters in a game of Scrabble. Products are like words: they are made by putting letters together, i.e. by stringing together the different bits of know-how that are involved in the process. Some words will be long and have many different letters like “Pharmaceuticals” while other letters will be short like “Hat”. Similarly, some products are more complex and require many different types of specialized workers to come together while others are less complex and require fewer specialized workers to join forces. There is an exponential growth aspect to this story. As the number of letters grows, the number of combinations grows exponentially. If you only have one letter, say the letter A, you can only make 1 word. With the letters A, C and T you can make 4 valid words in English and words that are up to 3 letters long. If you add the letter R, you can now make 9 words and words that are up to 4 letters long. With all 26 letters of the alphabet, you can make the entire English dictionary, which numbers in the hundreds of thousands. 

The Scrabble Theory of Development is the idea that societies become rich by expanding the knowledge that is embedded in its people and used in production that is increasingly diverse and complex. In the metaphor, it involves accumulating lots of different letters and expressing them in more words and, among them, in longer words. 

This metaphor gives us a way to measure how much knowledge has diffused in society. Places that have more letters should be able to make more words. So, the diversity of a place’s production should be related to how many letters it has. By the same token, the longer the word, the fewer the places that have what it takes to make it. We call the number of places that make a product, the ubiquity of the product, which is a measure of how long the word is – how much knowhow it requires. 

Following this logic, places that have more letters should be able to make longer words, i.e. less ubiquitous products. So, the average ubiquity of a place’s products carries information about how much knowledge the place has. Similarly, complex products should be made in places that have many letters, but if this is the case, then those places should be able to make many products. Hence, complex products should be characterized by the fact that they are made in highly diversified places. Hidalgo and Hausmann (2009) used this intuition to propose a metric of how much knowledge is in a place and in a product. They called the metric of knowledge in a place as the Economic Complexity Index (ECI) and knowledge in a product as the Product Complexity Index (PCI). 

In general, a place is complex if it is diversified, specialized in low ubiquity products, but those products are typically made in highly diversified places, that make low ubiquity products, and so on. Complex products have low ubiquity, are made in places that are diversified and specialized in low ubiquity products, and so on. 

21 

## **Cities and Economic Complexity** 

The Scrabble Metaphor centered around specialization and coordinating those specialized workers. This should be eerily similar to the story of the power of cities we told in the previous sub-section. This is no coincidence. In the Scrabble Story, cities are the physical places where specialized workers with different know-how can exist and be in physical proximity to one another so that they can come together in firms to make complex goods and services. Cities serve as platforms for collective know-how coordination, enabling firms to cluster together while connecting them to broader regional and global supply chains. The more sophisticated “letters” a city possesses, the more complex “words” it can spell, which determines both its current prosperity and its potential to diversify into new industries and export sectors. 

Our more recent research has allowed us to develop modified metrics of ECI and PCI based on those developed by Hidalgo and Hausmann (2009) to measure the complexity of cities (city-level ECI) and the complexity of products and industries located in cities (city-level PCI). See box below. 

These city-level metrics for ECI (for cities) and PCI (for industries) are powerful. Within a country, ECI at the city level is strongly correlated with both city size and wages (i.e., the urban wage premium). Furthermore, in at least some countries we have been able to show that places that are more complex than their populations would predict are likelier to have high population growth in the future and have higher wage growth in the future as well. 

If our measures of ECI correctly measures the know-how and therefore export capacity of a city, these results align strongly with what economic theory would predict. This is because for cities, success, especially in export ability, will show itself both in larger population (through in migration) and higher wages (through higher productivity).

On the other hand, this dynamic can create negative feedback loop where a city loses an export sector due to a shock (e.g., a city in an oil-producing region that faces an oil price shock). The initial export shock reduces the capacity to import and demand for nontradeable jobs, people start to leave and take with them their know-how, this reduces complexity which further reduces exports, creating a spiral. 

The same mechanism helps explain why complex cities tend to offer higher wages: with more advanced and diverse productive capabilities, they generate higher-value exports that command premium prices in global markets. Furthermore, the presence of more specialized letters allows workers to specialize even more and find other workers and firms that their specialization would complement most (Neffke, 2019). This relationship holds consistently across cities in the US: empirical data shows that cities with greater economic complexity reliably exhibit both larger populations and higher average wages, confirming how successful capability accumulation drives urban scale and prosperity.

## **Box 3. Calculating Economic Complexity Metrics at the City and Sub-national Level** 

This methodology adapts the established approach for measuring economic complexity in international trade to analyze cities and their industries. We start with employment data at the detailed industry level (NAICS 6 digits) for metropolitan areas across the country. For each city and industry, we calculate the Revealed Comparative Advantage (RCA) or Location Quotient (LQ), which measures whether a city has a higher concentration of employment in that industry compared to the national average. This reveals each city's relative specializations. 

We then apply a mathematical transformation that converts the RCA/LQ values to a scale from zero to one, capturing the full intensity of industrial concentration rather than using simple yes/no thresholds. These values form a matrix showing the relationship between all cities and all industries. 

From this matrix, we calculate two fundamental measures: Diversity (how many industries a city has, intensity weighted) and Ubiquity (how many cities have each industry, intensity weighted). We then normalize this data to account for differences in city size and industry popularity. 

Next, we construct similarity matrices that reveal which cities have similar industrial profiles and which industries tend to locate in similar types of cities. These similarities indicate shared underlying productive capabilities—the know-how that enables certain economic activities. 

We calculate the Industry Complexity Index (ICI) using a mathematical technique that looks for the most important hidden pattern in the network of industries that co-locate in cities. This technique analyzes the directions in which the industry-city structure varies the most, revealing a complexity gradient. The underlying principle is that complex industries require sophisticated productive capabilities, so they can only be found in cities that already possess diverse, advanced economic activities. Industries that appear mainly in highly diversified cities receive higher complexity scores, while industries found in many different types of cities (including less diversified ones) receive lower scores.

## **Cities and The Adjacent Possible** 

Following the Scrabble Metaphor further, expanding a city’s knowhow involves acquiring more letters and using them to make more diverse and longer words. But this process faces a chicken and egg problem: you cannot make the things you do not know how to make, and you usually do not know how to make the things you do not make. So, how do you start making new things if – to make them – you have to know how to make them, but you usually do not know how to make the things you do not make. 

This chicken-and-egg problem shows itself in the fact that diversification is not fully random. A place that only produces “HATS” will need to acquire only one letter to make “MATS”, as it already has the other three letters. But if, instead, it tries to make “LION”, it will need 4 letters it does not have – 4 chicken-and-egg problems –, while the letters that it does have are useless for that purpose.   Thus, the Theory of Economic Complexity predicts that places will tend to diversify from what they already know how to do to products and industries that are not too many letters away in order to save on chicken-and-egg problems. These things are what are in their “adjacent possible”[3] , building on what they already know how to do. Practically, this means that a city that already knows how to make women’s T-shirts will have a much easier time making men’s T-shirts than making a large jump to aerospace engineering. 

The idea of the “adjacent possible” implies that product and industries that are closer together in their required letters will be more likely to show up over time in the same cities. As mentioned above, Economic Complexity Theory predicts that a city which makes men’s T-shirts is more likely to jump into also making women’s T-shirt if these two products require similar letters. However, this implies that in the cross section of cities we are likely to see that cities that make men’s T-shirts will be more likely to also make women’s T-shirts. This means that we should see products and industries with similar “letters” co-locate more often. We can use this idea to measure how similar in know-how and letters two products and industries are. Those that are similar will tend to co-locate more, and those that have very different letters are in a sense more distant to each other and will co-locate less. 

We can use this measurement of how “close” or “distant” products and industries are to one another, to create a network showing the relationship between any product and all other products. We call this network the “industry space”.

In this space (which looks a bit like a thick forest), each node is a different product, and its links are to other products that are more closely related to it. Our research shows that countries tend to diversify to more nearby products (Hausmann _et al._ , 2014) We think of this like monkeys (workers and firms) in one tree (one node or product) being able to more easily jump to closer trees in the forest. Our new research suggests that this is also true for cities. City growth is also predicted by how much of the product space a city is close to and can more easily expand into over time. 

Thus, the process of economic development is the process of moving through the industry space starting with less complex products and industries and moving to adjacent more complex ones. This process is the process of accumulating more know-how through specialized workers and firms. 

Cities with a diverse and well-connected set of industries possess a broad knowledge base, enabling them to expand more easily into related and often more complex sectors. In contrast, cities focused on a few low-complexity industries tend to occupy sparser regions of industry space, making it harder to develop the specialized capabilities needed to enter more advanced sectors. Essentially, the industry space acts like a map, showing how close or distant new industries are. Moving into industries that are far away, requiring many missing capabilities, can be viewed as a long jump and is usually more challenging than expanding into nearby, related sectors. 

Following this map, economic diversification has some path-dependency: cities often (and especially over the long-run) build systematically on existing capabilities rather than leaping

arbitrarily into unrelated industries. This is also why we tend to find vertically and horizontally integrated clusters in cities. A metro strong in biomedical research, for instance, might attract venture funds, regulatory lawyers, and advanced-materials startups that complement that industry—like adding high-value consonants next to a cluster of vowels to form new words. Its research and development efforts will branch into adjacent fields from what it is already doing. This progression explains how Bangalore's early IT service firms paved the way for its current AI and biotech ecosystem: each new activity reuses part of the existing "alphabet" of capabilities while adding fresh elements, enabling cities to climb toward more sophisticated and valuable economic activities. 

Combine this with the positive feedback loop that export, and complexity have, and it means that, in many ways, cities diversify and grow the way crystals grow. Crystals grow with an initial seed that then is able to grow on top of this seed eventually being able to continue growing even after the seed is lost. Cities similarly may get started with some export sector which then attracts people, which brings in more know-how, which improves complexity, which increases exports and then brings in more people (see figure below).

This dynamic explains why some cities successfully reinvent themselves while others remain dependent on their original sectors. Both Colorado and Wyoming began as mining centers, but their trajectories diverged dramatically. Denver developed agriculture to sustain mining activities, which then became an export sector on its own. The city's growing population, central location, and transportation infrastructure attracted federal government institutions during the Cold War, including NORAD and the Air Force Academy (Leonard and Noel, 2016), which brought administrative and defense capabilities. This government presence then attracted aerospace industries that leveraged the region's

This capability building enabled Denver to achieve remarkable scale. Today, Denver's metro area has reached over 3 million people, creating the critical mass needed to support complex industries, while Wyoming remained primarily dependent on extractive industries. Wyoming's largest metro area, Cheyenne, has just over 100,000 residents, and the entire state contains only 588,000 people. Diversification also make cities more resilient to shocks. As discussed in the previous section, some industries do not like to collocate with other industries. Cities that specialize in these cities can be successful despite also being small, but they are likely to be successful in those isolated industries. This makes them more at-risk to shocks to the single industries they are specialized in. 

However, if a city has many sectors, a shock to any one of them is less likely to derail the whole economy. But even more important is how its people are specialized. While they are correlated, diversification and complexity are not the same thing. Complexity requires many different letters. If workers are specialized across a wide range of capabilities and not all in the same narrow task, it becomes easier for the city to adapt and develop new sectors when one is hit. If instead all the workers are trained to do the same thing, then the city can only support a narrow set of industries. And if that industry takes a hit—say, a low-complexity manufacturing process—they do not have many options. Those workers may find it hard to shift into other roles or industries, and the city itself may struggle to recover. 

But if a city is in a complex industry like high-end IT business consulting, it will have people and firms with a wide array of capabilities. Some will be strong in different branches of IT, others in business strategy, accounting, HR, software, and product design. Even if there’s a shock to the industry as a whole, those capabilities do not disappear. They can be recombined. A consultant might move into general business services. A software engineer might join a startup or shift into fintech. It is like Scrabble: if the city has lots of different letters, even if it loses one word, it can rearrange what’s left to form new ones. The real strength of complexity (and not just diversification) is not just having more but having more that can mix in new ways.

## **Box 4. Do Successful Cities Diversify or Specialize?** 

Think of a city's economy like a restaurant kitchen. Some kitchens specialize deeply – a sushi restaurant masters the art of raw fish preparation. ## **Serendipity and the Importance of Moving Brains** 

The process of diversification and know-how accumulation is fundamentally about the movement of brains through firms and people. It is when firms and workers with specialized know-how (i.e., letters) choose to locate in a specific city, that a city is able to then create more complex products (i.e., longer words). For countries this creates a challenge for diversifying into industries and products their populations do not already have the knowhow far. As we like to say, how do you train watchmakers, if you do not already produce watches? 

In the case of countries, accumulating letters you do not have is easier by moving brains. FDI and migration is often a source of new know-how that leads to diversification. The stories of this are plentiful. Robert Fortune was a British botanist who successfully broke the monopoly over tea growing by China in the 19[th] century. Considered one of the greatest stories of corporate espionage, Fortune’s journey had him go to China, sneak into tea growing regions, steal tea samples, learn tea growing techniques and try multiple times to grow tea in England and then in India. He failed many times despite his own knowledge, a good climate, and the technology and seeds he needed. He was only successful when he was able to bring tea workers from China with him to India and use their know-how. Today, India is one of the largest growers of tea in the world (Rose, 2010). 

Trying to attract catalytic FDI or migrants with the right know-how is often difficult for countries because they need firms and people to move across borders. However, as we will later touch on again in section 1.5, within a country people and firms can and will move between cities. Their reasons for doing so are not just based on pure maximization of income but a tradeoff across many different dimensions. Furthermore, they are more able to move and more willing do so in the sub-national setting. 

This mobility of people and firms means that the process of diversification is not purely deterministic at the city level. There is much room for serendipity and chance, though cities can implement policies that can help them have better luck. Serendipity and the mobility of know-how can enable cities to make dramatic leaps across the industry space when key entrepreneurs or firms choose unexpected locations. A single decision can transplant entire capability sets to unlikely places, fundamentally reshaping a city's economic trajectory. 

Bentonville, Arkansas exemplifies this phenomenon: Sam Walton built Walmart's retail empire in this small town largely because his wife preferred small-town living, transforming an agricultural community into the global headquarters of the world's largest retailer. Orlando's rise as the "Theme Park Capital of the World" followed a similar pattern when Walt Disney chose central Florida for its cheap land and climate rather than any existing entertainment capabilities. Las Vegas paralleled this transformation, leveraging state gambling laws rather than entertainment industry know-how to become "the Entertainment Capital of the World." These cases demonstrate how individual decisions—driven by 

30 

personal preferences, regulations, or geographic advantages— can create entirely new industrial clusters in places seemingly distant from relevant sectors in the industry space. 

This mobility of know-how through firm location decisions opens up important possibilities for cities seeking to attract businesses and accelerate their economic transformation. There is a crucial distinction between businesses that emerge organically from a city's existing productive ecosystem and those that might consider relocating from elsewhere, bringing their capabilities with them. 

Foreign direct investment (FDI) and firm relocation represent powerful mechanisms for capability transfer, as incoming companies transplant not just their operations but their entire knowledge base, supplier networks, and specialized workforce practices. Unlike organic diversification, which requires slowly building adjacent capabilities, attracting established firms can instantly introduce entirely new "letters" to a city's economic alphabet. This external pathway becomes particularly valuable for cities seeking to break out of low-complexity sectors or make strategic leaps into high-value industries they couldn't easily develop internally. 

But what determines which firms a city can realistically attract? The answer lies in understanding the specific needs that drive different industries' location decisions. Manufacturing firms seek to minimize costs and maximize efficiency, which draws them to locations with low labor costs, affordable land, and robust transportation infrastructure. BMW's choice to build its plant in Spartanburg, South Carolina, exemplifies this logic: the city offered significantly lower labor costs than traditional auto manufacturing regions, business-friendly labor laws, and proximity to a major port for global exports. 

Knowledge-intensive industries follow entirely different location logic, driven by access to talent and innovation networks. These firms gravitate toward locations with skilled workforces, strong universities, and high quality of life to attract employees. Amazon's selection of Arlington, Virginia, for its second headquarters was motivated by the region's large concentration of tech talent and proximity to government agencies that represent major customers. Service industries show varied but deliberate location preferences. Financial services firms, for example, balance cost efficiency with market access and regulatory advantages. This explains why JPMorgan Chase established a major operations center in Columbus, Ohio, to serve Midwest markets while accessing lower-cost labor, and why American Express built a large facility in Phoenix, leveraging cost advantages and a favorable business climate. 

At the same time, fundamentals and serendipity can interact in nuanced ways. Enrico Moretti tells the story of how Microsoft moved to Seattle (Moretti, 2012). The company started in 1975 in Albuquerque because its only customer, MITS, was there. Four years later, Bill Gates and Paul Allen wanted to go home, and they were struggling to recruit engineers to New Mexico, so they moved their startup to the Seattle suburb of Bellevue on New Year’s 

31 

Day 1979. At the time, Seattle was shedding jobs as Boeing (then the dominant employer) was conducting mass layoffs. In terms of its competitiveness and labor market characteristics, Seattle was very similar to Alburquerque. It was also “closer to today’s Detroit than to Silicon Valley” (Moretti, 2012: Chapter 3). Seattle’s business ecosystem was not the reason why Bill Gates and Paul Allen moved back home. 

However, as Microsoft grew, they quickly needed many trained engineers and workers and an ecosystem with a strong advanced university system. Due to its history with Boeing, Seattle had this available. Fast forward fifty years and the city has a strong innovation and tech ecosystem that creates many jobs across sectors. Seattle may have ended up having some sort of technology cluster because of its fundamentals and existing industries.  But it was Microsoft’s serendipitous decision ultimately that attracted thousands of programmers, venture investors, and other tech companies like Amazon. 

The story of how cities evolve their export sectors is ultimately a story about adaptation and opportunity. Cities grow like crystals, and like crystals, they can change direction, pivot to new industries, and even thrive after their original "seed" disappears. Denver no longer needs gold mines, New York doesn't depend on textiles, and Pittsburgh has moved far beyond steel. What matters is not the industry that started a city, but the capabilities it accumulates along the way—the knowledge, skills, and networks that become the building blocks for whatever comes next. Sometimes this evolution happens gradually, as cities move into adjacent industries that build on what they already know. Sometimes it happens suddenly, when an entrepreneur like Sam Walton or Walt Disney picks an unlikely location and transforms it entirely. 

The critical insight for policymakers and economic development practitioners is that urban economic transformation requires both nurturing organic growth from existing capabilities and strategically attracting external firms that can transplant entirely new knowledge bases. Cities that master this dual approach—building systematically on their strengths while actively attracting potential opportunities—position themselves not just to survive the loss of original export sectors, but to thrive through continuous reinvention. In this way, the modern city functions less like a static industrial machine and more like a living organism, constantly adapting its productive capabilities to find new pathways for growth in an everchanging global economy.

## **1.3 Cities are Labor Markets Where Letters Mix** 

## **The Relationship Between City Form and Function** 

In the last section we discussed how cities are the places where workers and firms with specialized know-how come together to produce. This means we might say that cities are fundamentally labor markets where people and firms benefit from being close to one another. In the words of Ed Glaeser, “Cities are the absence of physical space between people and companies (Glaeser, 2011). They are proximity, density, closeness.” (Glaeser, 2011: 15). This proximity allows firms to match with the workers they need and enables people to search for the jobs they want. 

The scale and density of cities are no accidents as they are a response to the productivity benefits of their labor markets. As Bertaud puts it, “without a functioning labor market, there is no city” (Bertaud, 2018: Chapter 2). The labor market function determines whether a city will grow or stagnate. Other factors, like the cost of living or amenities, are a part of the cities' labor market structure. People do not only work; they also need to have a life where they live. Yet, it is impossible to have one without income. 

In the Scrabble simile from the previous section, specialized workers and firms are letters in the alphabet. When cities function well as labor markets, people and firms complement each other’s productive capabilities. These capabilities are the “letters” of economic activity: the bits of tacit know-how that reside in people and organizations. 

Alone, a single work, like a lone Scrabble piece, can spell only a very short word; but when the worker mixes with others, they are capable of building longer words. Cities bring many diverse Scrabble pieces onto the same board. In that context, employers can assemble longer and more sophisticated products and services. Dense urban labor markets make Scrabble fast and cheap: firms are able to combine multiple capabilities that are present in the same city, and workers are able to find teams of people that help them progress in the labor market. The richer the local alphabet, the more sophisticated the menu of industries a city can sustain, and the higher the wages it can pay. 

The ability of cities to perform their function as labor markets depends on their physical form. The physical shape and form of a city will determine how well firms and workers can participate in the economic activity of the city. If it is easy for workers to get to work, firms to reach workers, and inputs to get to where they need to go, then a city will function more efficiently as a labor market. It is better able to not only produce more complex things but also better able to include all of its population in its economy. If it takes workers a long time to commute or they cannot afford to live close to jobs or amenities or the city is very congested, then the ability of the city to be the catalyst for complexity will be less. 

For example, long, expensive commutes will mean that workers are limited to working with others nearby to them.  As a result, they will likely work independently or in small firms at 

34 

much lower levels of productivity. In the language of Scrabble, this would be like letters forced to make only words with a few letters (i.e., low complexity) because gathering the letters to make longer words would be too expensive or difficult. Many of these factors like cost of living, ease and speed of commuting, connections between firms and workers with different parts of the city are all a result of the physical form of the city. 

Urban form is not just an outcome of architecture or planning decisions; it emerges from the interaction between market forces and policy **.** Markets set land prices and influence where people and firms locate, while policy or urban planning regulates what can be built and where. Together, they shape the spatial structure of a city. When functioning well, they create an environment where land is used efficiently, infrastructure supports mobility, and households can afford to commute to jobs, schools, and other places of social interaction. 

But when poorly designed regulations distort markets, cities can become segregated, inefficient, or unaffordable. In the absence of urban planning, markets can create adverse social environments like increased inequality, pollution, or traffic congestion. Thus, policy does not shape cities in opposition to markets, but through them. Bertaud (2018) shows that cities evolve not from central blueprints but from the decentralized decisions of millions of actors, and the urban planner’s job is to support those decisions, not replace them. 

Urban development is easier when policy is flexible enough to let the city grow **.** Cities that welcome new floorspace give incoming “letters” somewhere to land. Austin experienced a population boom led by tech firms that was possible because the city keeps adapting its urban codes to allow for smaller lots, taller buildings and faster approvals. By contrast, San Francisco has had relatively little new real estate developments during the same period due to multiple regulation layers, like environmental reviews introduced by the 1970 California Environmental Quality Act (Glaeser, 2011), that have squeezed supply, pushed prices above construction costs and diverted growth to other places. 

Besides markets and regulations, people’s location choices affect the shape of a city. Where households and firms locate is the result of a series of tradeoffs. Households trade off space for proximity or lower rents for longer commutes. You may choose a smaller apartment downtown closer to work and nightlife, or a larger house in the suburbs to have more space and an outdoor barbecue grill. These tradeoffs reflect not just income but also how people value leisure, floor space, time, and other relevant factors. Housing decisions also reflect the structure of the city: how well its transport system connects people to jobs, how rigid or flexible zoning is, and how housing supply responds to demand. 

In efficient cities, households can navigate these tradeoffs in ways that match their preferences. However, these tradeoffs become more painful when housing supply is constrained by regulations or infrastructure. These tradeoffs might also become difficult in the absence or deterioration of key amenities like schools, safety, or outdoor space. Finally, 

35 

they are determined by the availability and value of jobs. When these factors are constrained, households may find themselves without options and move to a different city. 

Regardless of how households and firms sort themselves, cities will always have central places (even in so called poly-centric cities). These are nodes of higher accessibility, where jobs, people, services, and amenities cluster. These centers minimize travel costs, so land in and around them is more valuable. As a result, cities will have a natural price gradient where land close to the “central nodes” of the city are more expensive but become less expensive with distance. This is the reason why land in Manhattan will be order of magnitudes more expensive per square foot compared to that in outlying suburbs.

However, not all cities follow the expected spatial structure where density increases with proximity to city center. And in almost every case, this is because regulations and planning have tried to go against natural urban and market forces. One striking example comes from South Africa, where apartheid-era spatial planning policies have left a long-lasting imprint on urban form. South African cities like Johannesburg exhibit fragmented, disconnected, and sprawling settlement patterns. The distorted urban shape of cities directly impacts their labor market function. 

Because many residents live far from job centers and transport options are limited or costly, the effective size of the labor market shrinks. The expected benefits of agglomeration are lost when people cannot access jobs within a reasonable commuting time. Shah and Sturzenegger (2023) argue that South Africa’s spatial structure have led to high transport costs and low informal productivity which then drive its poor labor market outcomes. In most developing countries, such spatial exclusion results in greater informality. But in South Africa, where low population density makes market access for informal workers more difficult, high transport costs result in persistently high unemployment. In this context, inefficient urban form actively undermines participation in the labor market and creates a structural barrier to inclusion and mobility.

## **1.4 Cities Compete and Interact with One Another** 

Until this point, we have discussed cities as individual units. In reality, cities and regions (urban and non-urban) within a country are an interacting, competing, interdependent set of places. When people and firms are able to move between cities and regions based on relative costs and benefits, cities and regions will be in competition with one another for firms and labor. Furthermore, cities and regions within a country are interdependent with one another as well as there are links through supply chains, markets, knowledge networks, and spillovers. This means that what happens in one city or region will affect what happens in another. Understanding growth in cities requires understanding how cities will grow and change as an interacting system. 

Within a country (but also sometimes across), firms and individuals are free to choose where they will locate. In making this choice, firms and individuals will make tradeoffs between various things similar to how they make tradeoffs of where to locate within a city. Individuals will care about which cities offer them jobs with high wages, how much it costs to live there (especially for housing and transport), the quality of schools, safety and crime, access to parks, community, weather, and other amenities that the city has to offer. Firms might care about how expensive wages are, the cost of utilities, the cost of land, access to markets, proximity to suppliers, taxes, and many other things. 

The key insight is that this choice of location is a relative one. An individual or firm will move to the city (region) that gives them the best trade-off between all the good things the city has to offer and how costly the city is to live in. If another city offers a better trade-off, and the costs of moving are not prohibitive, then people and firms will move to that city. If a city’s trade-off worsens, then people and firms will leave to other cities that offer a better one. 

When relative trade-offs between cities change, people and firms will move until it no longer makes sense to do so. In economics jargon, we would say that firms and people will move until the marginal individual is indifferent between where to locate. Or in more layman’s terms, in response to any change in one or more cities, people and firms will move until it is no longer in their interest to do so. This generates a natural resting state where no person or firm wants to move from where they are to another place. In the economics literature we call this state a “Spatial Equilibrium”. Shocks, both positive and negative, to any one city will shift the entire system of cities off of its “Spatial Equilibrium” but the movement of people and firms will generate natural forces that bring the system of cities back to another one. 

How might this happen? There are many benefits to density, proximity, and agglomeration. However, there are also costs. The more people and firms in a city, the more expensive that land and housing might become or the more congested the city’s roads might be. Or perhaps, the more workers a city has, the lower the wage level might go, or the more crowded public goods and spaces might get. 

41 

Thus, as one city becomes relatively more attractive, it will attract more firms and people, but this will make some other thing about the city worse (e.g., housing costs or congestion) until no one else would want to move in the city. Likewise in a place that has had a negative shock, people will leave until those that are left are the ones that still find the city an attractive place to live in. 

A key result of spatial equilibrium is that (after firms and people have shuffled around) if a city is better than other cities in some way, then this will be balanced by it being worse in some other way. For example, New York City is one of the most sought-after cities for firms and people to live in the United States because of its thriving economy, availability of jobs, relative safety, public transport, arts and cultural scene, etc. However, it is also one of the most prohibitively expensive cities in the world. In the United States, places with better weather (as measured by January temperature) tend to have lower wages for the same skill level and occupation (Glaeser and Gottlieb, 2009). This is because there will be individuals that care enough about living in a good weather place to accept a lower wage than they might get elsewhere. 

The ability of firms and people to move in response to shocks, growth, and decline in one city will affect growth in all cities. In the previous section we talked about cities as being labor markets where specialized workers and firms (letters) are matched to be able to make more complex products and services (longer words). In any labor market, we will have a downward sloping labor demand curve and an upward sloping labor supply curve (see figure below). The intersection of these two curves will determine the wage level (how much workers are paid) and the employment level (how many workers are employed). 

**Figure 14. Labor Demand and Labor Supply in a City**

sectors as well as in the non-tradeable sector through the local multiplier. Thus, if there is an increase in the demand for the city’s exports or if a city’s export sectors become more productive then we will see an increase (rightward shift) of the city’s labor demand.

How much employment and wages change will depend on the shape of the curves (a point we will be come back to later in this section).

We can think about the city’s labor supply curve being determined by how attractive the city is relative to other cities and regions of the country (including non-urban areas). In other words, the labor supply curve captures the trade-off in location for individuals described earlier that determine spatial equilibrium. 

If a city becomes more attractive because of better amenities like schools or cheaper because of more available housing, then this will increase (rightward shift) the labor supply curve as more people move into the city. This will result in a greater number of people being employed in the city, but at a lower wage. If a city becomes less attractive because of worsening amenities or becomes more expensive (say due to a lack of housing) then we would see a decrease (leftward shift) of the labor supply curve. This will result in fewer people being employed and a higher wage as people leave the city (see figure below) and firms increase wages to try and retain workers.

Remember that the labor supply curve of a city captures the relative attractiveness of the city and the trade-off workers make in locating in that city versus all others. Therefore, when the labor demand or labor supply curves in a city shift and people move, this will mean a shift in the labor supply curve for all other cities and regions where people and firms move to and from. 

For example, if crime worsens in a city this will cause the city to become less attractive and shift the labor supply curve in the city to the left. If a city or region is very small relative to the other cities and regions in the country, then the net effect on all other cities and regions might be very small as people and firms move to and from many different places. But if the city or region is very large relative to others then it will have an even greater impact on all other places. Even if the impact from one city to the entire country is small, it might be large for the regions and areas around it. 

One implication of the interdependence of city and regional growth is that success in a city, because it gets more productive, can show up as either increases in wages, increases in population, or a combination of both. If it is not possible for people to move into a city, its population will be fixed, and the labor supply curve will be a vertical line. In this case, a positive shock to labor demand will show up entirely in higher wages (and prices) and employment will remain the same. In the opposite extreme, if it is very easy for people to move into the city, then it is possible that labor supply will be very flat, and the productivity shock will show up as population and employment gains with very little if any changes in wages (see figure below). As we explain in Section III, this dynamic will be important for measuring success and diagnosing challenges at the city level.

As the figure and example shows, the shape of the labor supply curve determines whether changes in labor demand will show up in changes in population or changes in prices (especially wages). What determines the shape of the labor supply curve? First, costs and frictions for migration can make it more difficult for people to move into or out of a city. This will mean that changes in the city will result in fewer people being able to move in and out, and you will see more changes in wages and prices when there are shocks (e.g., the fortress city above). 

Second, how much housing is available for people and how easy it is to build more housing when people want to move in will determine how easy it is for the city to absorb additional population. We call this the housing supply elasticity as it measures how much and how quickly additional housing gets built as the population and housing prices increase. 

A higher housing supply elasticity means that the city can build lots of people in response to increases in population pressure on housing prices. This will mean that more people can migrate to the city if there is good news that makes the city more attractive, and it will mean that housing prices will not increase as much when more people move in. This will make the labor supply curve much flatter and result in smaller changes in prices (e.g., the flexible city above). In general, the more that the increased population crowds the city or makes it less desirable, the steeper the labor supply curve will be because fewer people can move into the city before it starts making the city a worse place to live in. 

Cities and regions within a country or province do not exist in isolation but instead are interacting and competing sets of places. Their growth also interacts with one another because of how firms and workers will choose to migrate. This means that we have to look at a city’s growth (or lack thereof) in the context of the country or region the city is in, in connection with all the other areas in that country or region. Decline in growth could be related to factors specific to a city, but they may also have to do with how attractive the city is relative to other locations. As we will show in the second part of this report, these interactions also give us a rich set of empirical tools to diagnose what might constrain

growth in a city. The other cities and regions around any given city offer a rich laboratory to understand growth and how to unlock it.

In this way, growth in the city can spill over to regional or national growth in two ways. First, when people are able to move into the city more people in the region or nation will be included in the growth process. In addition, even for those living in other places wages will rise, making them better off as well. However, for this to occur people must be able to move into growing, successful cities. If they are unable to because of migration restrictions or housing constraints, then the benefits of growth will simply make prices rise in the original city benefit a few people in the city (especially landowners), and many fewer people will be included in the growth process. In a 2019 paper, economists Chang-Tai Hsieh and Enrico Moretti calculated that greater housing supply in US larger super-star cities would have allowed many more people to live and work and benefit from those cities’ growth.

Aggregated, total US GDP by 2009 would have been at least 3.7% (or more than USD 3500 per worker) higher (Hsieh and Moretti, 2019). 

This story is the classic story of structural transformation in developing countries where growth in formal sectors and urban areas (in non-agricultural industries) raises wages for the entire country as more people from rural areas move to the city to get higher wages in the expanding formal sector. At the same time, productivity and wages in rural areas also increase. Thus, especially for developing countries, cities growth crucial for broader economic development and structural transformation (Fields, 2004). 

City growth can be regional or broader national growth, and it can be inclusive growth, but people and firms have to be able to participate in order for the nation to benefit. 

## **1.6 What Does a Successful City Look Like?** 

In this first part, we have looked at cities from many different angles and have presented five important ideas related to cities and city growth. Specifically: 

1. Exports are key to sustaining a city’s ability to import and its demand for labor. 

2. Cities grow and diversify their exports in a process of accumulating know-how through workers and firms. 

3. Cities are fundamentally labor markets, but their physical form will determine their ability to perform this function, and a city’s physical form is a result of a complex interaction of markets, regulations, and individual choices. 

4. Cities grow as interconnected and interdependent sets of places and so must also be attractive enough to bring in firms and workers. 

5. When cities grow and people and firms are able to participate in that growth, then the entire region and nation can benefit. 

When a city is successful and thriving it will have strong, dynamic export sectors built on lots of specialized workers and firms and know-how. It will be a place that has flexible and efficient transport and housing that allows workers and firms to meet, and therefore it will be an attractive place for firms and workers to locate. Lastly, a successful city will be one that helps the entire region or country to grow. While this may seem like a tall order for any city, the history of cities and economic growth has been exactly this. Using these building blocks, we can now build a diagnostic framework to understand when cities are struggling and what to do about it.

## **2.1 What is Growth Diagnostics?** 

Economic output requires production inputs. This applies to countries, cities, and all sectors. Making physical products, like in manufacturing, mining, or agriculture, requires inputs such as raw materials, machinery or land. Services also require inputs, like physical capital (e.g., hardware) or specialized abilities acquired through education, experience, or both. Most importantly, production inputs are not limited to capital and labor; they can also include a variety of factors such as public goods (e.g., physical infrastructure, an education system, etc.), proximity to supply chains, transportation systems, or housing stock. 

The inputs required for a given economic activity function as complements instead of substitutes. Any business requires many inputs that are not interchangeable. You cannot use a car to do the job of a tractor, and you cannot ask a back-end software developer to do the job of a designer and expect the same quality of output. Similarly, you can have a fintech start-up with one brilliant coder, but nothing ships if the cloud connection drops, or the compliance lawyer cannot clear the regulatory maze. You also need electricity, so you cannot make up for a blackout with extra software engineers. In all, the specific input needed cannot be substituted by a different input, in the way a new factory does not ameliorate the problem of intermittent electricity for a manufacturer. 

51 

The scarcest inputs determine the speed of economic growth in a place. The fact that inputs are complementary means you run out of some inputs before others; when that happens, production will be constrained by the scarcest input. If you run out of coffee, you will want to pay a lot for coffee, but it won’t make sense to buy a lot of sugar. The Growth Lab has long illustrated this with Liebig’s barrel,[4] the bucket whose volume is capped by its shortest stave, not its average length (Figure 23). For a city, that slab might be a congested bridge, a lack of investment attraction capacity, a conservative zoning code, or a sheriff who cannot guarantee safety after dark. Lengthening any other plank, for example, investing in more entrepreneurship programs when safety binds most, will fill no extra water until that critical slab is mended. 

**Figure 23. Leibig's Barrel** 

The growth diagnostics framework is a toolkit intended to identify the most binding constraints for a given economy, that is, the most needed inputs. Classic policy checklists treat problems as substitutes. Cities that try to follow every recommendation at once often miss the shortest slab. Standard policy formulation tends to focus on inputs that appear to be in low supply or are problematic, without considering whether they are truly holding back growth. A superficial process of policy prioritization thus fails to identify the input that is both scarce _and_ highly demanded by economic actors. Growth diagnostics flips the question from “What is missing in general?” to “What is binding here and now?”—the one slab that, if lengthened, lets the barrel hold much more water.

## **2.2 Understanding a City’s Growth Challenge Based on Its Trajectory** 

It is difficult to imagine a city without change. Cities go through periods of expansion, they rise out of nothing, they collapse very quickly, and they create new opportunities in times of need. Change remains constant in cities. In his short story _The Immortal_ , Jorge Luis Borges imagined a city without change. Its population did not grow nor decline, because its inhabitants were immortal. In their immortality, they became troglodytes that forgot language and do not sleep. They lived in caves carved by “time rather than industry.” 

We have not figured out immortality yet, so we are subject to time. Like history, cities reflect how humans deal with time. Cities that fail to evolve eventually fade, as stagnant industries lose their edge. Industrial hubs rely on constant reinvention, since firms in tradable sectors compete globally and must adapt their production to stay competitive. In the long term, urban space shows big changes cities have gone through, sometimes in the form of ruins from a distant past, other times in the form of eloquent architecture. In the short term, a city’s main variable of change is its sheer number of people. 

The population dynamics of a city say a lot about its economic challenges. A booming city will attract immigrants looking for job opportunities. A city facing economic hurdles will lose people to places that are more affordable, more attractive, or simply have more opportunities. In contrast, the income dynamics of a city say less about its urban success (Glaeser, Scheinkman, and Shleifer, 1995), as it only partially reflects productivity growth. A city might have experienced income growth because it became less attractive, hence workers demand higher wages to live there. In contrast, the development of new and more productive industries might attract lower-wage workers in ancillary industries (restaurants, basic services) that would net out any per capita income effects. 

Another reason why the population growth of cities is a key success metric is that urban economic development without expansion is only possible in a fortress city. A city creating new economic activities that raise productivity and bring more income per capita will increase the demand for low-productivity workers in non-tradable industries like services (e.g., restaurants, barbershops, nail salons, dry cleaners). The only way a city can become richer without expanding its population is by not letting people in. As a result, the city 

53 

becomes more and more expensive, as the residents demand better services that they might have to go get somewhere else or can only be provided on a scale and not by small shops. In turn, the lack of restaurants or small shops becomes a disamenity for which workers demand compensation, as nobody wants to live in a city without restaurants or cafes. 

The population dynamics of cities seem to follow mysterious laws that we do not fully understand. Research has shown that, with exceptions, the size of cities within countries tends to follow Zipf’s law, which states that the size distribution of cities fits a power law (Gabaix, 1999).[5] This means that the biggest city in the US is two times larger than the second biggest city, three times larger than the third city, four times larger than the fourth city, and so on. This pattern is consistent over the past century, which tells us that urban population growth follows another mysterious law: Gibrat’s law of proportionate effect. This law states a city’s growth rate is independent of its size. Evidence shows that there is no significant difference between the average growth rate of small cities and big cities or, in other words, cities’ growth rates are random. Cities rise and fall, but there cannot be two Tokyos and there cannot be no Tokyos in Japan. 

The growth rate of cities is random relative to their size, but that does not mean that cities cannot grow faster or better, nor that they cannot go through population decline. In fact, the volatility of growth rates is higher for small and medium cities than for large cities. Growth is random in relation to city size, but there is a myriad of factors that can make a city thrive or decline. Although it is difficult to establish general laws that cities follow, we can identify growth drivers or constraints on a case-by-case basis. But how much should a city aspire to grow? When is growth constrained? When is growth sustainable?

## **Mapping a City’s Tradable Growth Drivers** 

Understanding a city’s growth trajectory requires examining the sources of tradable income that sustain economic life. As discussed in Section I, tradable income is the oxygen of a city. Once it enters, it circulates through the economy, supporting both the tradable and nontradable sectors, as well as enabling a city’s ability to import goods, attract services, and sustain employment. The level and composition of tradable income determine whether a city can grow, how it grows, and whether that growth can be sustained over time. 

To understand how the process of growth happens in a city, we must map the specific channels through which tradable income flows in. This includes identifying which industries sell goods and services to customers outside the city. Private investment into real estate or businesses is another source of tradable income, as are remittances from migrants. Public transfers are a significant source as well, whether to the individual in the form of safety net payments and public sector wages, or to local government via fiscal transfers. A final source is commuter income, which often takes place within a commuting zone but is important for jurisdictions within a larger metropolitan area. These sources, taken together, form the base of income inflows into a city that enables job growth and imports. 

The structure and dynamics of tradable income evolve over time. A city that gains competitiveness in an expanding tradable sector may experience a boom. The inflow of 

58 

income attracts capital and labor not only into the exporting firms but also into the local services and amenities that support them. Over time, however, if these firms fail to innovate or if their sector loses global relevance, the city may lose its competitive edge. When this happens, the decline in tradable income causes ripple effects across the economy. Cities in this position often stabilize at a lower level, relying on public transfers and other passive inflows. While they may continue to function and provide a baseline quality of life, they are no longer growing from within. 

Not all tradable income sources follow boom-bust cycles. Some sectors provide consistent, if modest, inflows. Tourism, government employment, stable legacy industries, and the steady presence of commuters or in-migrants drawn by amenities can serve as long-term stabilizers. These sectors do not necessarily lead to rapid growth, but they do ensure a degree of economic resilience over time. 

The central question is whether the city’s current sources of tradable income are sufficient to sustain its growth trajectory. One way to assess this is to look at whether tradable inflows are translating into population growth, wage growth, or rising housing prices. As shown by Glaeser, Gyourko and Saks (2005), productivity gains in cities tend to show up in one or more of these three outcomes. These indicators serve as proxies for underlying economic vitality. If none of them are increasing, it is likely that tradable income is flat or declining. The previous subsection on benchmarking a city’s growth trajectory focused on population growth as a signal of economic shocks and relative success, helping situate this indicator within broader national and regional patterns. However, increased productivity from tradable income inflows can also manifest in rising wages and housing costs, not just population growth.

## **Characterizing a City’s Labor Market**

It might be the case that people from Amatenango enjoy the incredible amenity of living in the mountains, living off from what they cultivate, and staying connected with their traditions. It might also be the case that the people from Teotitán require relatively high wages in order to bear with the tourists that visit Oaxaca. 

The variations in the wage and population levels are informative when characterizing a city’s labor market. The workers in the city of Morelia in Michoacan, for example, earn wages that are 5% lower than the national average, while wages in Hermosillo, Sonora, are 30% higher. Does that mean one city is underperforming and the other one is succeeding? It’s hard to tell which one is what. Maybe the cost of living is higher or the amenities worse in Hermosillo, which is why workers require higher wages. These variations indicate that factors beyond mere scale, such as living costs or amenities play crucial roles in determining urban economic performance as measured in the wage premium. 

We must put a city's trajectory in the broader context of how its labor market compares with other places in terms of quantities and prices. Population growth alone doesn't tell the full story we need to understand a city’s trajectory. A place might be growing rapidly, but if it's a small city with lower-than-expected wages, this suggests factors other than job opportunities are attracting residents. Conversely, we might observe a city growing slower than average but discover it's a large metropolitan area with high wages where housing supply is spatially constrained, limiting population growth despite strong labor demand. Understanding whether wage premiums align with city size, as economic theory predicts through agglomeration effects, helps us identify cities where growth patterns deviate from expectations. This context is crucial for diagnosing whether observed growth reflects healthy economic dynamics or points to underlying market distortions that need to be addressed.

## **Formulating a Growth Question** 

A grounded understanding of a city’s growth trajectory and current position sets the stage for formulating the growth question. The growth question is the guiding inquiry of the Growth Diagnostic. The growth question identifies the city’s central challenge and directs the diagnostic process toward resolving it. Growth questions often frame the city growth challenge in terms of low growth, population decline, non-inclusive growth, unsustainable growth, highly variable growth, or the challenges of managing and maintaining existing growth. The following are brief examples of growth questions; each illustrated with a city case to show how different trajectories provide a distinct framing to the central growth challenge of a city. 

## _**Growth is Low: The Case of Cali, Colombia**_ 

Cali, the economic anchor of Colombia’s Pacific region and the country’s third largest city, illustrates the challenge of persistently low growth. Despite its strategic location just inland from Buenaventura, one of Colombia’s largest ports, the city has underperformed relative to peers. 

From 2014 to 2024, Cali’s population grew by just 6 percent, far below Colombia’s national growth of 15%. Among the country’s major cities, Bogotá and Medellín grew by 14% to 15%, while coastal peers on the Atlantic side grew even faster: Barranquilla 20%, Cartagena 15%, and Santa Marta 21%. This divergence suggests that Cali has been unable to capture population inflows and dynamism that have fueled other Colombian metros. Labor market outcomes reflect the same pattern. The Consejo Privado de Competitividad reports Cali’s 

62 

metropolitan unemployment rate at 10.96% (2024), lagging peers such as Bogotá at 10.40%, Barranquilla at 10.41%, and Medellín at 8.99%, and only on par with Cartagena at 10.95%. 

At the same time, real estate dynamics complicate the picture. From 2016 to 2025, new housing prices in Cali rose by 126%, outpacing Bogotá at 85%, Medellín at 122%, Barranquilla at 84%, and Cartagena at 68% (DANE – IPVN). Instead of a straightforward story of economic stagnation, Cali shows a puzzling combination of sluggish labor markets and surging housing costs. 

The contrast between the metros in the Pacific and Caribbean regions of Colombia has been highlighted in the media. The Economist described Cali’s region as the national laggard in a 2019 article titled “Why Colombia’s Pacific Coast is So Poor,” pointing to inflexible land systems and difficulties in infrastructure development. By contrast, Barranquilla on the Caribbean coast has been lauded as an “economic miracle” by the Colombian think tank Fundesarrollo and others. 

Cali’s trajectory therefore raises a pivotal Growth Question: why has a city with a large population, strong housing demand, and strategic port access failed to translate these assets into robust demographic and economic growth? Addressing this low-growth puzzle requires uncovering the binding constraints that limit Cali’s ability to leverage its geography and scale into broad-based economic dynamism.

## **Box 9. What are the Boundaries of a City?** 

In all the discussions we have had so far, we have assumed that there is some boundary that determines what is a “city”. While this is convenient and useful from a theoretical point of view, it is more complex in practice. Furthermore, the frameworks and methodology we propose can be just as easily applied to any subnational place (e.g., region or Megalopolis of different interacting metro areas). 

One way of thinking about a city is by considering what might be considered a coherent labor market. Usually this will be done using commuting, residential, and job patterns. Commuting zones or Metropolitan Statistical Areas (MSA) in the USA and other countries are an example of one such aggregation. Areas that are functionally part of the same labor market generally operate together in one system and so make sense to analyze as an economic unit. 

However, the challenge with such a definition is that it is often not aligned with political or administrative borders where policy operates. For example, the commuting zone or Metro Area may include a city core which administratively is a single district but then also include many suburban towns and other areas where people work and commute from. These areas are tied together in one labor market, but policy agents are fragmented. 

Still, it is often useful- even when working on an aggregation level different from the entire labor market of the Metro Area or commuting zone- to compare the dynamics for individual political and administrative units and the whole labor market. For example, in the United States, there is a common phenomenon for many Metro Areas, where the city core is depopulating while population in the suburbs and thus the Metro Area as a whole is increasing. This is the situation that is occurring in Baltimore City vs Baltimore MSA that includes Baltimore City and the surrounding county. Baltimore City has been losing population even while the MSA as a whole has been increasing in population. 

In this situation, you might have one growth question (and thus diagnostic) related to the Metro Area as whole, one for the specific city core, and separate questions for the different towns and areas in the wider county. Still, analyzing multiple levels is very helpful in the diagnosis.

## _**A Challenge of Inclusion: The Case of Savannah, GA**_ 

Finally, some cities experience strong economic growth yet face inclusion challenges. Savannah, Georgia, is a prime example. In 2024, the city finds itself in the midst of unprecedented economic expansion as the first electric vehicles are rolling off the assembly line of the brand-new Hyundai Motor Group Metaplant America (HMGMA). Three years ago, Hyundai announced a $5.5 billion investment in electric vehicle and battery manufacturing facilities, the largest single investment in Georgia's history. This transformative investment triggered a cascade of economic activity, including an influx of new residents and secondary investments from Hyundai suppliers seeking proximity to the plant. 

Savannah's growth stems from its competitive advantages in tradable sectors, low cost of living, and exceptional quality of life in what is undeniably a beautiful city. For two decades, economic developers had pursued an Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM), watching other cities across the Southeast secure automotive investments. Now that they have successfully landed HMGMA, the city's challenges have fundamentally shifted. Rather than worrying about growth or competitiveness, Savannah faces the problem of ensuring that the benefits of this economic boom reach all residents. 

Despite the influx of investment, significant disparities remain. The area includes places where the population remains deeply disconnected from economic opportunities. In Bulloch County, which encompasses part of Savannah's wider region, more than 25% of the 84,000 residents live below the poverty line, just a 30-minute drive away from HMGMA. The city’s focus now is on translating growth into inclusion, so that all communities can benefit from this economic transformation. 

## _**Concentrated Growth Drivers: The Case of Carmen, Campeche, Mexico**_ 

Ciudad del Carmen illustrates the risks of a city dependent on a single sector. The weakness of its economy stems from a lack of diversification, with offshore oil refining and a single off taker, Pemex, dominating its tradable base. As Mexico’s offshore oil hub, Carmen’s growth trajectory has closely mirrored swings in its sole sector. In the 2000s, Carmen’s metropolitan population grew at a compound annual rate of 2.54%, reflecting the boom years of oil production (OECD Data Portal). Growth slowed to just 1.19% annually in the 2010s, as production matured and opportunities elsewhere in Mexico and abroad became more attractive (OECD Data Portal). 

The 2020s have highlighted the fragility of this model. Oil production in the Cantarell and KuMaloob-Zaap fields have already passed peak levels, and Pemex itself has become financially unstable. Since 2021, the company has stopped paying many local suppliers, worsening the strain on Ciudad del Carmen and Campeche as a whole. The demographic effects are clear: from 2021 to mid-2025, Campeche’s employment base shrank by 1.6%, while Mexico’s as a whole grew by 7.6% (INEGI). Local economic activity has deteriorated sharply: Carmen’s airport, once hosting up to eight flights a day, now operates only two; 

65 

hotels report minimal occupancy; and the Chamber of Commerce estimates sales have fallen by as much as 70 percent among its members (Energy Magazine, 2025). The sectorspecific downturn has led to a city-wide recession. 

This dependence underscores the vulnerability of an economy reliant on a single export for its tradable base. Carmen and Campeche remain exposed to long-term decline as oil fields deplete and Pemex’s financial problems persist. For policymakers, the key growth question is not just how to reignite growth—largely dictated by global commodity cycles and resource depletion rather than local policy—but how to diversify Carmen’s economic base. The pivotal Growth Question is how Carmen and Campeche as a whole can build new, more stable sources of tradable income that provide economic resilience beyond the oil sector. 

## _**Maintaining Growth: The Case of Bangalore, India**_ 

Bangalore illustrates the opportunities and challenges of a city that has become a global hub for knowledge-intensive services. From 2000 to 2018, the city’s population grew at a compound annual rate of 4.07%, outpacing major Indian peers such as Chennai (2.59%), Delhi (3.37%), Hyderabad (2.92%), Mumbai (1.19%), and Kolkata (0.64%) ( _World Urbanization Prospects 2025: Summary of Result_ , 2025). By the 2020s, Bangalore had solidified its reputation as the “Silicon Valley of India.” In 2024, the city attracted 47% of India’s $12B+ startup funding (Startup Genome), with investment directed not into basic back-office functions but into advanced sectors such as space tech, artificial intelligence, and cleantech. Bangalore is now the world’s second-largest AI talent hub, employing more than 600,000 AI/ML professionals. The city’s tradable base continues to diversify and move up the value chain, providing high wages and consolidating its global role in the knowledge economy. 

Despite this momentum, concerns are growing about the sustainability of its growth trajectory. Infrastructure has not kept pace with population and investment. Bangalore is consistently ranked among the most congested cities in India (World Bank, 2018), and urban amenities have deteriorated as green cover steadily declines (Kaushik, Bhandari and Kay, 2025). While growth has not yet faltered, rival cities such as Hyderabad are seeking to lure businesses and talent with lower costs and modern infrastructure. 

Bangalore’s growth trajectory remains strong. The key framing is how the city can continue to deliver economic opportunities across diverse, sophisticated industries while ensuring quality of life for its growing population. By investing now in tackling emergent constraints, the city can prevent future barriers from actively restricting economic activity. The pivotal Growth Question is how Bangalore can sustain its global leadership in innovation and maintain long-term growth by addressing emerging constraints to growth before they actively hamper the city’s successful growth trajectory.

## **Summary of Growth Trajectory Analysis** 

In summary, understanding a city's growth challenge requires a careful reading of its population and economic trajectory. While urban growth may appear to follow mysterious patterns, cities are neither immune to decline nor guaranteed to thrive. Their growth trajectory is shaped by multiple factors that require in-depth and case-by-case analysis. Charting a city’s economic journey helps define the right growth question. As illustrated in Figure 26, cities with low growth must ask, “What constrains current growth?”; those reliant on narrow sectors should ask, “How do we make growth resilient?”; cities where growth is not inclusive must explore, “How do we grow through inclusion?”; and those with no immediate growth problems should look ahead and ask, “What might constrain growth in the future?” Each question leads to a different type of growth diagnostics. 

The principle is to keep the growth question simple. What matters is accuracy, not complexity. A growth question should not embed a presumed solution but instead frame the problem in a way that allows the diagnostic to uncover growth constraints. The following stage in the diagnostic process continues by asking “why?” at each step until the binding constraints on growth are revealed. Formulating a growth question is the first step in shaping inclusive and sustainable urban economic growth.

## **2.3 The Growth Diagnostic Tree** 

Now we need to find answers to our growth question. The city growth diagnostics decision tree is a heuristic framework to identify where the binding constraints are and should not be taken as a definitive set of concepts. What follows is a toolkit intended to help the growth diagnostics analyst find those answers following a series of analytical steps. We present this toolkit in the form of a decision tree, which is a framework that models an order of decisions that should be made when diagnosing growth problems. It is a first approximation intended to help practitioners and applied researchers find answers for complex problems. We 

67 

unpack the questions and issues that are essential for answering our growth question and follow a logical order in solving them. We discuss the evidence that is required to move along the nodes of the tree towards finding the binding constraints in the following sections. 

The city growth diagnostics analysis follows a series of steps: 

1. **Analyze the city’s growth trajectory** . We start by looking at whether the population of the city has been growing compared to other places. If the population has not been growing, we might be able to identify when it stopped and what events were associated with that. If the city has been growing, we should ask about other relevant economic indicators that show what drives the growth, like industry composition, immigration, and urban expansion. 

2. **Analyze a city’s tradeable growth drivers** . We identify sources of tradable income in the city and their evolution, as they will inform what’s the growth potential and current problems. 

3. **Characterize a city's labor market** . We complement the trajectory analysis with information on its labor market size (total population or employment in relation to other cities) and prices (wages or wage premium). 

4. **Formulate a growth question or challenge** . We do a city growth diagnostic to understand how to achieve inclusive, resilient, and high-quality growth in a specific city context. Yet the specific focus of this question varies significantly across different urban circumstances. For cities experiencing population loss, the focus is identifying current constraints to economic growth, while cities that are already growing may need to focus on anticipating future constraints and achieving sustainable and/or inclusive growth. Has growth been inclusive? What are the main drivers of economic growth? 

5. **Identify whether the main problem stems from firms’ capacity to create job opportunities (labor demand) or people’s desire to stay in or relocate to the city (labor supply).** Cities are fundamentally labor markets, so we can examine if challenges affect growth through labor supply or labor demand channels. Labor supply factors directly affect individuals and their location decisions as workers and household members. The demand-side factors directly affect the ability of tradable firms to compete and grow in markets outside city boundaries. 

## **When we observe signals of labor demand problems:** 

6. **Determine whether binding constraints are discouraging existing firms from investing and creating jobs** . A process of population decline or stagnation might be associated with external or internal factors. External factors are shocks that cannot be controlled like global industry trends (e.g., Detroit, USA), natural disasters (e.g., Acapulco, Mexico) or national demographics (e.g., Japan). Internal factors are associated with the availability of different production inputs that can be constrained, 

68 

like traffic congestion affects the availability of workers. Shift-share analyses (Bartik instruments) can help disentangle local dynamics from external factors. 

7. **Establish whether growth is or might be constrained by the lack of new firms or economic activities.** If existing firms are growing well, but there is no diversification, then the city might be facing problems down the road. Research indicates that cities’ economic growth is strongly associated with their capacity to create new industries and economic activities (see Section 1.2). In most cases, the diversification bottleneck lays in difficulties to solve coordination problems associated with new investments or innovations. 

**When we observe signals of labor supply problems:** 

8. **Assess whether the city faces challenges that make it unaffordable or unattractive for residents and potential in-migrants (cost of living)** . We first examine housing costs to see if housing supply is constrained. We then analyze whether transportation costs make the city unaffordable **.** Transportation can severely affect a city’s cost of living through multiple channels, including housing costs, commuting times, or congestion. 

9. **When a city remains affordable yet struggles with growth, we investigate what makes it unattractive** . In Section 1 we introduced the concept of “amenities”, which is where we now look for binding constrains. Amenities are difficult to quantify, compared to other relevant factors for economic growth in cities, but there are usual suspects that we can examine. A city might be unattractive for workers when there is low or no provision of public goods (low quality of schools or public safety issues), when the natural conditions are not favorable (extreme climate or lack of natural amenities), or when there is a scarcity in the supply of recreational activities (e.g., restaurants, sports facilities or cultural scene). 

After determining what the bindings constraints are, two last steps complement the previous analytical process: 

10. **Identify an economic growth syndrome based on the constraints analysis we’ve done so far** . We now investigate how and why the binding constraints persist in the city. Why have they not been solved? At this point, we’ve covered all potential sources of problems for economic growth in the city. We go back to the growth question or challenge we formulated in Step 2 and make sense of it using evidence on binding constraints. The answer to those questions will be the syndrome, that is, the collection of symptoms that indicate a persistent condition the city must overcome to achieve better economic growth. 

11. **Design policy interventions to address binding constraints** . The main purpose of growth diagnostics analysis is to inform policy priorities to achieve better economic 

69 

growth. The last step of the process is to examine potential solutions for the constraints that were identified in the previous steps. This step is relevant because prioritizing the wrong policy solutions can lead to counterproductive results. For example, if the city’s most binding constraint is the lack of public transportation corridors, but stakeholders decide to promote housing developments in the periphery, the pressure on mobility will be worse.

## **Labor Demand vs. Labor Supply Constraints** 

A growth diagnostic analysis starts by asking whether a city’s challenge most affects labor demand or supply, because cities are fundamentally labor markets. A city competes in two markets: A market to sell its exports outside of its boundaries, and a market to attract residents and workers to live in the city. The demand for the exports of a city determines the amount of labor demanded in both the tradeable and non-tradeable sectors. Labor supply is merely the city’s workforce, determined by the factors that affect its ability to attract and retain workers, like its cost of living and its amenities. 

Each city has an equilibrium wage where the total labor demanded equals labor supplied (Figure 28). We can infer how the labor market is performing in a city by looking at changes in prices and quantities. As we showed in Section 1, changes in prices and quantities in a city should be analyzed in relation with the rest of the urban labor markets. Additionally, wages or income per capita are difficult to interpret by themselves. We cannot say whether a city is facing demand issues if wages are rising or falling. Wages can hide more than what they show. But together with population changes, they can reveal a lot.

Any shock that results in a new equilibrium of wages and employment reverberates across both sides of the market. A city's problems never affect only workers or firms; they always

affect both. For example, a deterioration in transportation infrastructure makes it difficult for people to commute to work and school. It also makes a city less attractive for firms that might prefer other locations where transportation is cheaper and faster. The same applies to housing or amenities. A manufacturing company looking for new sites will pay particular attention to housing prices and availability. In turn, the lack of labor demand will make a city less attractive for workers looking for job opportunities. 

Nevertheless, constraints become binding for economic growth through either demand or supply channels. Although they determine the equilibrium wage and employment, they do so by affecting either the labor demand or supply curves. In that sense, housing, transportation, and amenities _mainly_ affect labor supply, and, _secondly_ , labor demand. In turn, production inputs specific to firms, such as reliable electricity, logistics services, or access to intermediate goods, _mainly_ affect labor demand, and _secondly,_ job opportunities for workers or labor supply.

We utilize nominal instead or real wages because they are more responsive to changes in demand or supply. If a city has a positive or negative demand shock, real wages may not change if the population (and thus the demand for housing and non-tradables) is able to adjust quickly. While nominal wages will still change, measured real wages will not. For example, if a city is facing a decrease in real wages, we wouldn’t know if that’s because housing costs increased (labor supply shift) or because there are less job opportunities (labor demand shift). In turn, we can also use population instead of total employment in a city, because in most cases they are closely correlated, although there are exceptions (Box 10). 

The movements in wage and employment levels respond to shocks that shift one or both curves producing a new equilibrium wage. Labor demand shifts when a shock changes firms’ need for workers, holding wages constant. For example, a new technology that makes workers more productive may increase firms’ demand for labor, while a decrease in consumer demand will produce lower demand for labor. Labor supply shifts when a shock changes workers’ willingness or ability to work, holding wage constant. For example, an increase in female labor force participation will result in more labor supply, while a decrease in working-age population (increase in aging population) will reduce the supply of labor. Movements along the curves without a shift in the other curve are always temporary, while new equilibrium outcomes are due to shocks that shift the curves.

## **Box 10. The Nature of Spatial Equilibrium at the National Level** 

The diagnostic tree described in this section is most valid in the situation in which labor market mobility can enforce a dynamic of spatial equilibrium. This requires: 1) people being able to move across cities to take advantage of job opportunities and 2) wages and local prices that are able to adjust to economic conditions. The combination of these two things allows for all shocks and changes at the city level to reveal themselves through changes in population/migration combined with changes in wages/local housing prices. 

It is important to check whether spatial equilibrium holds at the national level. When there is a shock in labor demand, do population, wage, or local prices adjust? In some places, for instance, prices may not be market determined and are not allowed to adjust. If people are able to move, then spatial equilibrium will still be a good framework to use. However, adjustments may not show themselves in wage or price changes. For example, if wages are rigid because of minimum wage laws or union bargaining, then changes in labor demand will not show in the wage level. However, they will result in changes in the employment rate. In these cases, you would want to use the employment rate rather than wages along with population changes to determine if there is an issue with labor demand. 

To see whether spatial equilibrium holds, you can look at a simple scatter of cities or regions within a country where the population (level or change) is on the x-axis, and the second where the employment rate (e.g., for the working age population) is on the y-axis. In a “normal” spatial equilibrium world where wages can adjust, you should see a relatively flat, tight relationship, where the employment rate does not vary all that much with city size in levels or changes.

- a. **Positive labor demand shock with inelastic (low) labor supply.** In this scenario there are two potential cases to be made. In the first case, we have a city that experiences growth in economic activity but struggles to attract workers because of factors that increase the wage workers would expect to live there, hence reduce their reaction to smaller wage increases, like disamenities or housing costs. In this city, we would find a tight labor market (low unemployment). For example, a city where firms want to locate but workers find it difficult because housing is limited by strict regulations. 

   - The second case to be made in this scenario is that the city is facing no real constraints to labor supply, but high productivity growth. After all, tech hubs grow in expensive places. Many cities experience increasing housing cost as population, density and productivity grow. There is a “physical” limitation in terms of how many more people can fit in a big city, due to building technology (we don’t build too high or too much below ground). In that sense, it is expected that Manhattan’s housing costs are high, and the supply curve is less elastic than in other places. There is also a productivity effect on the elasticity of supply. The places that become more productive require and host more expensive amenities and services, which in turn increases the value of housing. As cities become more productive, the elasticity of their supply curve decreases, but that is not because of any binding constraint or problem that could be solved by policy. 

- b. **Positive labor demand shock with elastic (high) supply** , means a city will face strong population growth coupled with (lower) wage growth.  This city faces no apparent constraints that limit economic growth. However, the typical challenge in this situation is to manage growth well and ensure quality of public services.

- **(2) When population is increasing and wages are rising slower than average or decreasing:** 

   - a. **Positive labor supply shock with elastic (high) demand** increases population more than wages. The positive shock in the supply curve means that the city became more attractive or less costly compared to other places in the country, hence more people are willing to work there given wage levels. In turn, the fact that the absolute change in wages is smaller than population growth means that the demand for labor is elastic, which in this case means that firms respond with more job opportunities to the decrease in wages produced by a larger workforce. In this case, there are no apparent constraints that prevent the labor market from growing. 

   - b. However, we should analyze changes in the city’s sectoral composition. For example, retirement or consumer towns might not show increases in wages even when there is an increase in tradable income coming from the retired population. The retirees come to spend income from past earnings to the city, but that won’t show up in higher present wages. The city will experience an expansion of labor demand in non-tradables or tourism industries. 

   - c. **Positive labor supply shock with inelastic (low) demand.** The city will experience moderate population growth with a significant decline in wages relative to the national average. The increase in population pushes wages down, but labor demand is relatively inelastic, meaning firms are not willing to create more job opportunities with minor wage decreases. For example, the demand for nurses in each region is constrained by scale, as hospitals have a very specific number of nurses they hire regardless of their wages. If there are not enough nurses, the wages will rise, while if there are plenty of them, wages will go down, but the number of job openings won’t change because demand is relatively inelastic. Similarly, a city in this scenario might be facing potential demand constraints other than scale that make demand inelastic. For example, a city might face an inflow of immigrants due to increased connectivity, attractive amenities or low housing costs, but find it difficult to expand economic activity because it’s already a relatively low-income place with low-complexity industries. In this scenario, there are limited job opportunities, which may indicate constrained labor demand, and an increase in the low-income population. 

- **(3) When population is decreasing and wages are rising slower than average or decreasing:** 

- c. **Negative labor demand shock with inelastic (low) supply.** In this scenario, the change in wages is larger than the change in population. This would be the case of city that is losing jobs or firms, but where people still want to stay despite large nominal wage cuts. The population does not respond to wage cuts because the city is still relatively

attractive, or migration is restrained. For example, a city that specializes in an industry that goes through a negative shock, like San Francisco during the dot-com crisis in 2000, might see a decline in wages and employment in that high-paying industry, but lower population decline. In fact, San Francisco lost 17% of high-tech employment between 2001 and 2008, but population only decline by 1.4% in 2000-2004, only to recover its rising trend thereafter. Multiple factors might keep people in place during those times, including homeownership or amenities such as being part of a community. In this case, regardless of the elasticity of supply, the scenario indicates binding constraints to labor demand. 

- d. **Negative labor demand shock with elastic (high) supply.** The city experiences a large population decline with a lower decrease in wages. This indicates that the population is sensitive to wage changes, which would be due to wages being low relative to the cost of living and amenities. A few firms leave the place, so a lot of people decide to follow. Like in the previous case, this scenario presents a strong signal of constraints that produce negative labor demand shock. However, in this case, we should analyze what makes labor supply elastic, because that might indicate additional problems. Because population decline is large, we could see a negative spiral effect, where a negative labor demand shock leads to additional constraints to labor supply. For example, we could see rising crime due to a lack of jobs. It could also be the case that we face an ambiguous scenario in which the constraints that affect labor demand are the same factors behind supply elasticity. A city with a deficient transportation system might push both people and firms away. 

## **(4) When population is decreasing and wages are rising faster than average:** 

- e. **Negative labor supply shock with elastic (high) demand.** In this scenario, people leave even though there are job opportunities in town. As a result, wages increase but in a smaller magnitude than the decrease in population. This indicates that supply is constrained by factors that are pushing people away, but firms are still willing to pay higher wages, so demand does not seem to be constrained. This would happen if the cost of living or disamenities push people away in search of higher real wages. For example, if a city faces a crime wave or a deterioration in public services like education, the same wage will be more attractive somewhere else. In turn, this might also lead to a negative spiral where economic activity declines. 

- f. **Negative labor supply shock with inelastic (low) demand.** In this scenario, the city faces moderate population decline with a significant increase in wages. The situation is like the previous scenario, only firms are willing to pay even higher wages for workers not

## **2.4 Principles of Differential Diagnosis** 

We now have classified the type of city that we are analyzing into labor-demand vs laborsupply constrained and have a much better idea of where the problems are in our city. 

However, the next step in our growth diagnostic tree is to test which specific constraint is binding, in other words, why is labor demand lagging or why is labor supply unresponsive? Labor demand constraints and labor supply constraints can originate from many distinct drivers. The idea of testing for binding constraints is inspired from “differential diagnosis” in medicine. In differential diagnostics, we recognize that there is a list of many potential symptoms that map to different diseases (i.e., potential constraints). However, diseases can share symptoms, and therefore we need to focus on the symptoms that can help us differentiate one disease from each other. 

Fundamentally, differential diagnosis for binding constraints rests on the idea that if a constraint is binding it should show up in many different symptoms some of which will help us differentiate it from other potential constraints. The key question to ask is “If X is a binding constraint, then what should I see as a consequence”? In general, we should come up with specific tests for constraints we think might be especially binding, and these tests might be very specific to the constraint in question. 

If a constraint is binding, it should pass one of five tests: 

- 1) Magnetic fields: People that move away from a place with a constraint go to a place without that constraint 

- 2) The (shadow) price of the constraint should be high. 

- 3) Changes in changes: Movements in the constraint should produce significant movements in city growth. 

- 4) Agents in the economy should attempt to overcome or bypass the constraint. 

- 5) Camels and hippos: Agents less intensive in that constraint should be more likely to survive and thrive, and vice versa. 

The exercise of differential diagnosis and testing of constraints is not meant to be a definitive causal inference study. Each individual test by itself may not be definitive in determining whether a constraint is binding but together with the evidence that the specific branch of the tree is binding can be strong evidence. If a constraint is binding, then we should see a combination of these tests occurring. 

81 

## **1. Magnetic fields: Agents that move away from a place with a constraint go to a place without that constraint** 

A powerful way to identify constraints is by looking at what causes people or firms to leave a place. Cities compete with one another for firms and workers, so looking at what pushes firms and workers away from city A and towards city B can inform what constrains city A. If the push factor in city A is inversely related to the pull factor in city B, then it is likely a binding constraint. For example, if people leave a violent city for safer places, violence is a binding constraint. Violence acts as a repulsive force, while safety works as a magnetic field. 

When firms move from a city with high taxes to a city with lower taxes, we can say that’s a sign that high taxes are a constraint. This can happen within a metro area or between districts that belong to the same broad region. For example, if people are moving from a place that has no amenities like restaurants, parks, or cultural activities to ones that do, then that is a sign such amenities are a constraint. 

Comparing the city in question with the places its resident move to is the most indicative test of binding constraints, but data is often unavailable. City level migration matrices are not always accessible nor frequently updated. A simple comparison of multiple factors associated with population growth might be informative of broad patterns at the national level but is unlikely going to shed light on constraints for specific city cases. When data is available, analyzing how push and pull factors play out in spatial equilibrium is an important test that the growth diagnostics analyst can conduct. As we explained in Section 1, people and firms vote with their feet, so the constraints analysis starts with tracing where they are going. 

## **2. The (shadow) price of the constraint should be high.** 

The second test for a binding constraint checks whether low quantity is present alongside high prices. When evaluating whether a specific input is a binding constraint, a low quantity does not necessarily indicate scarcity. For example, just because water usage is low among firms may not mean that it is a binding constraint. Water usage might be low because the firms do not demand water to be productive. If water is a constraint, then we should see low usage alongside high commercial or industrial prices for water. This would suggest that water is a scarce resource, and firms are willing to pay a large amount for access. In general, when there is an issue of low labor demand and we want to see what type of input most constraining productivity in export sectors is, the price test can be one of the most revealing. 

In many cases, we might not have a direct measure of the price of an input, so we would want to look for “shadow” prices. Shadow prices are essentially alternative measures of scarcity or abundance when there does not exist a direct price or if official prices are not set by a market. For example, there is no market nor price for the use of public roads. However, scarcity of road access will show itself instead in congestion and high travel times along 

82 

those roads. This congestion or travel time figure is like the price of the road and indicates that this is a scarce, in demand resource. 

The most common types of shadow prices are those that involve rationing, queues, wait times, or over-subscription for access. We can often discover a constraint specific shadow price when non-market means are not used for allocation by looking at the specific allocation mechanism. For example, if access to schools in a city are determined by a lottery and good schools are scarce, then we will likely see oversubscription or longer wait lists for some schools over others. 

## **3. Changes in changes: Movements in the constraint should result in significant changes in city growth.** 

It is hard to explain change with a constant, and so if a constraint is binding when that constraint moves, so should growth in the city. Often when we see a city or place get into trouble, we will see a change in its growth trajectory, especially its population. This might be an explicit fall in city growth, or it might be a slowdown in growth. In general, if a constraint is binding it should have become worse around the time (and before) of the fall or slowdown in growth. Conversely, if the constraint was loosened at any point, we should see that city growth increased afterwards. For example, if we think that crime is a binding constraint, then we might see that an exodus of people from the city coincided with an increase in the crime rate or we might see that when the crime rate went down, we saw an increase in the population. 

## **4. Economic agents should attempt to overcome or bypass the constraint.** 

Finding solutions for binding constraints results in high economic returns. Therefore, we should see residents and firms in the city trying to get around the constraint, often in creative ways.  For example, if electricity is a constraint for firms in the city, you might find many firms in the city using generators or solar panels. One of the key challenges in a city diagnostic context, however, is that agents may try to overcome or bypass the constraint simply by leaving. Therefore, there may not be as many agents who stay given the constraint, but those that do will likely have creative means of addressing the constraint that those that leave did not. 

## **5. Camels and Hippos: Agents less intensive in that constraint should be more likely to survive and thrive, and vice versa.** 

When a constraint is binding, we should see those least affected doing better than those that are doing worse. If a city is very remote and lacks transport linkages to other places and therefore is limited in what exports it can produce, then the industry composition of the city will look different than a well-connected city. As an analogy, in a dessert you will see plants like cactuses and animals like camels that are very efficient at conserving water. This is a 

83 

sign that water is scarce in this environment. Conversely, a place with hippos will most likely be one that is abundant in water. 

## **2.5 Diagnosing Constraints to Labor Supply and Labor** 

## **Demand** 

Once you have identified whether your city’s growth is characterized by a lack of labor demand or labor supply, the next step is to diagnose the specific constraint within that typology. Each constraint, whether related to jobs, housing, transport, or amenities, will show up differently in the data and in lived experience. The following sections walk through the most common labor demand and labor supply constraints, applying the principles of differential diagnosis introduced earlier. For each potential constraint, we use heuristic tests: magnetic fields, shadow prices, changes in changes, camels and hippos, and getting around the constraint. These tests are not definitive on their own, but when used together and interpreted in context, they offer a powerful way to identify what is truly holding your city back. 

## **Labor Demand Constraints** 

Labor-demand constraints occur when a city’s firms cannot generate or expand jobs fast enough. Firms either earn tradable income by selling goods and services outside the local labor market, or they serve local consumers and businesses through non-tradable activities. But even non-tradable sectors ultimately depend on the spending enabled by tradable income, which brings money into the city. In sum, tradable income is the fundamental driver of labor demand in cities. 

Tradable-sector firms rarely exist in isolation. Instead, they tend to cluster in industry hubs to benefit from agglomeration forces, access to specialized labor, knowledge spillovers, proximity to suppliers and other factors. Since tradeable sector firms are not dependent on local demand, they concentrate where these advantages are strongest. Clustering lowers costs, boosts productivity, and fosters innovation. But when these conditions are weak or missing, tradable industry hubs may struggle to form or grow, limiting the city’s ability to expand labor demand. The presence and growth of industry hubs, rather than isolated firms, is what enables cities to sustain resilient inflows of tradable income and, in turn, support lasting job creation and economic expansion. Cities play a central role in enabling the growth of industry hubs by creating the conditions that allow tradable-sector firms to cluster, scale, and thrive. Growing a hub requires policy that supports hub formation, including high-velocity labor markets, access to suppliers and end markets, shared public goods like local branding, and reliable access to shared production inputs. 

Labor Demand constraints affect: 1) a city’s existing firms and industries or 2) the city’s ability to diversify into new economic activities. In the first case, the constraints can be external shocks to the tradable sector or missing production inputs. In the second case, 

84 

diversification is mainly constrained by the city’s capacity to resolve coordination failures. In this subsection, we first examine how external shocks can become binding constraints and why diversification of the tradable sector is central to economic resilience. We then turn to firm-level production inputs, distinguishing between vertical constraints - (industry specific needs such as power, water, or regulations) and horizontal constraints (broad necessities like permitting, roads, and public services). Finally, we explore how coordination problems can constrain diversification into new activities, thus limiting the city’s economic resiliency and long-term growth prospects. 

## _**External Shocks**_ 

No city is immune to global events beyond local control that can suddenly depress labor demand in key tradeable industries. Technological progress that wipes out entire industries or occupations, global trade shocks, or the relocation of global supply chains can become constraints for a city’s economic prospects. These are problems that affect the tradeable sector outside city boundaries and are not in the control of the local stakeholders. This was the case, for example, of the China shock for many cities in the US and Mexico. When China started to compete with North American manufacturing firms for the same market, many towns suffered from fast deindustrialization. 

There is not much that cities can do to reverse this process once it starts. However, they can create conditions to increase their resilience to external shocks, mainly by diversifying the number and type of firms in their local tradable sector. If a city’s tradable income depends on one or a few firms, it's much more sensitive to potential external shocks that might affect those firms than a city with a more diversified set of firms in the tradable sector. In that sense, the best thing a city can do to strengthen its economic resiliency is to promote diversification and business creation. 

When it comes to differential diagnostic tests, in this case we want to see if the shocks affecting the city’s industries are external or exogenous to local factors. When external shocks are the constraint, we would expect the industries or firms that are affected by them to face economic downturns (hippos), while the rest of industries or firms are not affected (camels). Bartik shifts analysis can help disentangle these trends. Although economic research on identifying demand shocks has evolved, the basic intuition of Bartik shifts analysis is to distinguish between local and national trends controlling for industry composition. For example, the automotive industry might be facing problems at the national level due to global factors, so my city will be affected by them if it’s part of the automotive supply chain. On the contrary, if only my city is facing a decline of employment in the automotive industry, then this is likely not because of external shocks but because of local factors affecting the industry. 

Examples of testing for external shocks using differential diagnostic tools include: 

85 

## **Changes in changes** 

- Breaks in local employment or output trajectories line up with dated, exogenous events (e.g., tariffs, technology adoption, relocation of supply chains). 

- Cities more exposed to external shocks underperform matched peers with similar local fundamentals. 

## **Camels and hippos** 

- Exposed tradables (hippos) contract while locally sheltered sectors or unrelated tradables (camels) remain stable or grow. Within-city industry mix shifts away from the affected value chain. 

- Occupations specific to the shocked industry see larger a larger decline in wage premia or employment than other occupations. 

## **Getting around the constraint** 

- Firms pivot from export to domestic markets, alter product mix, or re-target geographies. 

## _**Production inputs**_ 

Production inputs at the firm level can also affect a city’s economic growth. In a broad sense, firms require various inputs to produce goods and services. A subset of those do not vary across cities. For example, macroeconomic stability or political risk can be considered production inputs since they are essential for a firm to maintain normal operations. In some countries, macroeconomic issues can quickly become binding constraints on economic activity, but they would affect all cities within a country to the same extent. When doing a growth diagnostic at the city level, we are interested in the production inputs affecting firms locally and within city boundaries. The inputs existing firms require can be specific to an industry or type of firm (vertical) or affect all firms alike (horizontal). 

Vertical inputs vary across industries and types of firms. Targeted vertical inputs especially matter for strengthening industry hubs. For example, vertical inputs can be electricity or water infrastructure for industrial real estate, or regulations that impede the development of that specific industry in that region, like environmental requirements. The nature of these constraints often depends on the industrial composition of the city's tradeable sector. A port city specializing in petrochemicals will face different vertical constraints than a mountain town focused on tourism or technology services. Electricity reliability might be critical for data centers and manufacturing plants, but less binding for consulting firms or creative industries. Similarly, water quality standards have become essential for pharmaceutical 

86 

manufacturing, but may not affect software development clusters. We could think of human capital or skills as a production input that could be constrained, but because cities are small open economies (Section 1), firms could always attempt to bring workers from other places. If there is a skills shortage in a city, it is likely due to labor supply constraints or a skills shortage in the whole country. 

As in the case of external shocks, testing for constraints in vertical inputs can leverage a “camels and hippos” type of analysis. We would expect to see that the firms that are more intensive in the input are struggling and the firms that are less intensive in that constrained are not facing any performance issues. For example, if the city faces issues in the electricity sector that result in outages, high prices, or both, then the firms that are less intensive in the use of electricity will be less affected, and vice versa. We could also apply an “agents bypassing the constraint” type of test. For example, if there is a shortage of industrial real estate or site development due to a lack of engineering capacity, we might see some manufacturing firms developing that capacity themselves. 

Horizontal inputs are standard for all firms within a city. They can be, for example, public inputs that the government provides and might be affected by a lack of state capacity. If a city has problems providing construction permits, this will likely affect all businesses regardless of their industry. Other horizontal inputs include functioning land registries, road infrastructure, and waste management. In practical terms, a two-year delay in approving a real estate expansion has the same chilling effect on a software firm waiting for office space as on a food-processing company looking for new sites. 

Testing for horizontal constraints might be more challenging, as the ability to compare economic outcomes between industries or firms within the city would not necessarily yield insights, since horizontal constraints affect all firms and industries alike. However, we can apply still find ways to extract insights from descriptive analysis. A “camels and hippos” type of test can still be insightful since some firms might still be less intensive in the use of that specific input than others, hence less affected by it, so we would expect to see more of them than those that rely on the input more heavily. For example, some cites have specific income taxes that affect all economic activity, but if those taxes are restrictive for growth, we would expect to see more informal activity than in other places, especially in industries with lower margins. 

Some examples of testing for production inputs include: 

## **High shadow price** 

- _Vertical inputs:_ Expensive or unreliable power or  water (tariffs, outage losses, backup generation costs); scarce or over-subscribed specialized facilities (e.g., wet labs in biotech or anechoic chamber in electronics and other manufacturing industries) with waitlists and premium rents. 

87 

- _Horizontal inputs:_ Long permitting or land-registry queues, high logistics dwell times, chronic congestion (time “price”  of roads), steep industrial land rents near serviced sites are signals of citywide bottlenecks. 

## **Changes in changes** 

- Output, investment, and plant openings rise after discrete improvements (new substation, water trunk, interchange or port expansion, permit digitization) and stall when these degrade. 

- Neighborhood or corridor-level “on/off” patterns: firms  boom where a new service is delivered first, then replicate as  service expands. 

## **Camels and hippos** 

- Sectors intensive in the constrained input struggle (energy- or water-heavy manufacturing, cold-chain food processing) while less-intensive services or light assembly grow. 

- Within sectors, plants located on the “served” grid outperform similar plants off-grid or at the end of overloaded  lines. 

## **Getting around the constraint** 

- Private workarounds: self-generation, private roads, in-house  permitting teams, on-site warehouses, or relocating within the metro to serviced industrial parks. 

- Collective workarounds: industry associations pooling testing  or certification or shared logistics 

## _**Coordination Problems**_ 

In turn, a city’s diversification process into new economic activities can be constrained by coordination problems, which are closely related to a city’s ability to access specific inputs. A promising industry may struggle to grow because it requires investments that are not happening because their demand is not there. These types of problems can only be solved through effective coordination among private and public stakeholders. For example, a beautiful beach town might have a nascent tourism industry that is unable to grow because the town is not well-connected to larger cities within or outside the country via air or road connectivity. The development of airports or road infrastructure often requires active coordination across stakeholders. 

The coordination problems affecting diversification are “chicken & egg” problems. What comes first? The supply of inputs or the demand for them? For example, a region might have the potential to produce certain agricultural crops but lack the capacity to test and develop plant varieties because it lacks an agricultural research institution. The agricultural sector, 

88 

along with local governments, would want to coordinate to create an institution that can provide the necessary know-how for agricultural diversification. 

Testing constraints for diversification is difficult because we are trying to understand what potentially limits investments that have not yet happened. Some examples include: 

## **High shadow price** 

- Scarcity premia for inputs: Very high wage premia for specific occupations or expensive specialized services (tooling, design for manufacturability) 

- Missed opportunities: Repeated investments lost because of  the same missing complement (e.g., lack of flight connections or  industrial sites). 

## **Changes in changes** 

- Changes in firm creation or export products following arrival of an anchor firm, a shared facility (pilot plant, testing lab), or  new transport connections. 

## **Camels and hippos** 

- Standalone activities with not many complementarities (back-office services, basic assembly) appear; activities that  require clusters, suppliers, or agglomeration (biotech, med-devices, advanced components) fail to take root. 

## **Getting around the constraint** 

- Pioneers vertically integrate to internalize missing complements (e.g., in-house testing, supplier development) 

- Emergence of coordinating vehicles: PPP industrial parks, public-private roundtables, export-promotion platforms, university-industry lab evidence that agents are trying to solve the chicken-and-egg problems 

## **Labor Supply Constraints** 

A city can face a binding constraint to growth through labor supply as firms cannot hire workers as needed. The city becomes unattractive for immigrants or current residents because there is something in the total compensation package that makes it not worth it. The city may be facing excessive crime, natural disasters, a decline in the school system, or a rise in living costs due to housing shortages, all of which would lead workers to demand higher wages in return for living there. In turn, the city becomes less competitive for firms looking to relocate or for current businesses wanting to expand their operations, as firms face higher labor costs or even workforce shortages. 

89 

## _**Housing**_ 

If a city is facing an issue of labor supply, the next step is to confirm whether the city faces housing supply constraints or if the city is unattractive for other reasons. When the growth diagnostics analyst finds indications of labor supply constraints, then the next question becomes, what is making people demand higher wages or causing them to leave despite those wages? The key idea is that people must be demanding higher wages to offset something they do not like about the city or are unable to move in to take advantage of those higher wages. 

One way to assess whether housing supply is constrained is to examine the responsiveness, or elasticity, of supply to changes in demand. In cities where supply is inelastic, even modest increases in demand can drive up prices or push people into informal housing arrangements. Additionally, in cities with low elasticity of housing supply, positive demand or productivity shocks benefit landowners more than any other local agent (Moretti, 2010) through increases in real estate value. 

Signs of inelastic housing supply include: 

- **Magnetic fields** 

   - Residents moving to cities with lower cost of living to do the same or a very similar type of job 

   - Residents moving within the city to areas with lower housing costs and willing to pay other costs like congestion or disamenities 

## • **High shadow price** 

- Rapidly rising housing prices in formal markets indicate a high shadow price for housing. Demand is strong, but supply is not expanding. 

- High housing prices relative to incomes reveal deteriorating affordability and a housing market misaligned with local earning capacity. 

## • **Changes in changes** 

- Relaxation of housing regulations in specific areas leads to a spike in new developments 

## • **Getting around the constraint** 

- Prevalence of informal settlements or slums shows people bypassing the formal market due to affordability or access barriers. 

- Worsening housing quality, overcrowding, or reliance on aging stock reveals that households are adapting to inadequate supply by making do with substandard options. 

- Lower-skill workers may be pushed to live farther away and face long commutes from peripheral areas where housing is more affordable— evidence that local housing constraints are reshaping labor market access. 

90 

High or rising prices, especially when high relative to incomes, or the growth of informal housing typically reflects unmet demand. As mentioned in Section 1, the presence of slums or informal housing is usually a sign that housing supply is constrained in the places people want to live and work. In a responsive market, residential construction firms would expand supply to take advantage of high prices. When they are unable to do so, due to zoning, infrastructure, land, or regulatory constraints, it signals a supply inelasticity that may constrain urban growth and affordability. 

There are two key constraints that reduce housing supply elasticity: land use regulations and radial infrastructure. Land use regulations, such as minimum plot size zoning, floor area ratio (FAR) limits, parking requirements, and height restrictions, can impede housing supply growth by capping allowable density, introducing complex approval processes, and increasing compliance costs. Several signals can help identify when land use regulations are binding. These include sharp increases in housing prices without corresponding increases in density; housing construction bunching right at allowable zoning thresholds, suggesting developers would build more if permitted; lengthening wait times for permits or project approvals; low housing density in central neighborhoods compared to peer cities; faster growth in housing supply outside of the municipality but still within the commuting zone; sudden increases in housing construction following zoning changes; and the presence of workaround solutions like accessory dwelling units or informal conversions, which indicate that formal channels are too constrained to meet demand. 

Housing supply inelasticity can also stem from a lack of radial infrastructure (i.e. the arterial public goods needed to make land viable for build-out). The absence of trunk lines for water, sewer, or stormwater drainage can prevent the conversion of peripheral land into housingready parcels, even if zoning allows it. These systems, along with roads, electricity, and broadband internet, are often publicly provided and determine whether land is effectively usable. In some areas, restrictions on density are necessary in the absence of sewage and water systems, as higher population concentration could lead to aquifer depletion or septic contamination. Several indicators point to infrastructure constraints. If developers are required to install trunk lines themselves as a condition of development, this often reflects a public underinvestment in infrastructure. A large price difference between land with infrastructure access and land without it may indicate that infrastructure is a limiting factor. In some areas, homes rely on wells, generators, or septic systems, even in urban or periurban zones, because public systems do not reach those areas. And stark differences in housing density between neighborhoods with and without infrastructure access can reveal how infrastructure availability shapes the pattern of urban expansion. 

## _**Transportation**_ 

A lack of functional transportation can act as a binding constraint on urban economic growth by limiting access to jobs and reducing the benefits of agglomeration. While a metropolitan area may appear integrated in population statistics, it can function as a 

91 

fragmented economy if workers cannot reach major employment centers. Commuting across the city becomes a burden, especially for lower-income residents who live farther from job centers. These residents may require compensating wage differentials to justify long or costly commutes, or else withdraw from formal employment altogether. In such cases, the effective labor market is far smaller than the total urban population. 

Transport systems, whether public transit or road infrastructure, are essential for connecting people to jobs, schools, services, and social opportunities. When these systems are inefficient, such as with non-functioning transit or chronically congested roads, commuting becomes burdensome. Poor transport access becomes a structural barrier to labor market participation, agglomeration, social mobility, and inclusive growth. Several diagnostic tests can help identify whether transport is acting as a binding constraint: 

## • **Magnetic fields** 

- Residents move away from congested areas within the city, willing to pay for other costs like housing or disamenities 

- Residents move to cities without transportation issues to do similar types of jobs 

## • **Shadow price is high** 

- Transport costs (both in time and money) are disproportionately high, especially for formal workers 

- Significant differences in employment and informality rates between neighborhoods based on access to transportation. 

- Business complaints about labor shortages, despite low local labor force participation rates. 

- Indicators of inefficiency such as low average driving speeds or high congestion levels. 

## • **Changes in changes** 

- Neighborhoods that receive transport upgrades often show improvements in labor market indicators and vice-versa, 

- A declining share of public transit use over time due to worsening service quality or accessibility results in a smaller labor market, hence firms finding it more difficult to hire workers. 

## • **Camels and hippos** 

- Well-connected neighborhoods attract high-margin service sectors that commute daily for work. 

- Peripheral areas with poor transport access see more informal retail, homebased enterprises, and self-employment. 

## • **Getting around the constraint** 

92 

- Workers relocate closer to jobs, leading to higher housing prices or higher densities in well-connected areas. 

- Workers rely on informal or time-intensive transport options, such as motorcycles, shared vans, or minibuses, and ask for a wage premium to do so. 

- Some workers reduce labor force participation or shift to locally available, lower-wage, or home-based work to avoid long commutes. 

The case of Nairobi shows how transportation constraints can limit city growth. Transport challenges are symptomatic of a broader pattern in rapidly urbanizing Sub-Saharan African cities, where infrastructure provision lags population growth. Kenya’s urbanization rate has risen from 20% in 2000 to 30% in 2024, and Nairobi’s population has more than doubled over the same period from 2.2 million to 5.5 million. As the city’s population swells, congestion intensifies, and the lack of efficient public transport forces residents to rely on slow or costly commuting options, distorting labor markets. Figure 33 shows that Sub-Saharan African cities perform poorly on average in terms of vehicular speed,[8] and Nairobi stands out for having lower transportation speeds per city size compared both to global peers and slightly below the Sub-Saharan African average, which indicates a high shadow price for transportation. This mismatch between urban expansion and mobility infrastructure reduces the city’s functional size. While Nairobi may cover a vast area on the map, the effective economic geography is compressed into pockets within reasonable commuting distance of major job centers.

## _**Amenities**_ 

A city may also be facing a binding constraint that affects its amenities. Amenities are factors that make the city have a better or worse quality of life, considering living costs and wages. In urban economics literature, amenities are difficult to define, as they can encompass a wide range of things, from weather to recreational activities like attending the theater. In the framework of spatial equilibrium, changes in amenities principally affect labor supply: when amenities improve, workers are willing to accept lower wages or higher housing costs in exchange for better quality of life, while deteriorating amenities require higher wages to retain residents and still risk prompting outmigration. Economists refer to this adjustment mechanism as compensating differentials, where wages and housing prices move to balance differences in amenities across places. 

Amenity levels are not fixed. They can change over time, either gradually or through technological or societal shifts. For example, advances in air conditioning made extremely hot regions like the U.S. Sunbelt more livable, helping them attract new residents. More recently, the rise of remote work has allowed people to choose where to live based on quality of life rather than job availability, leading to migration toward scenic or touristoriented areas like mountain towns or exurbs even though these places were previously considered remote or peripheral. Similarly, once-overlooked neighborhoods or cities can gain amenity value if they become safer, cleaner, or more culturally vibrant. Urbanist and economic geographer Richard Florida, in his book _The Rise of the Creative Class_ , argues that cultivating vibrant neighborhoods, cultural assets, and urban amenities can spur creative industries by attracting talent, in turn reinforcing a city’s long-term economic dynamism (Florida, 2019). 

A change that produces disamenities or reduces the quality of life for residents can become a binding constraint on growth. For example, if the city faces deterioration of its public safety or schooling system, or adverse effects of climate change, it will become less attractive. A 

95 

town might also lack a competitive service industry, which would show up as a negative factor as people want to be able to access recreational and cultural services. 

Diagnosing amenity-related constraints requires interpreting urban trends carefully. Signs of constraints in amenities include: 

- **Magnetic fields** 

   - Residents move to cities with higher housing costs, hence lower real wages, because are pushed away by disamenities that affect their quality of life 

   - In cities with high amenities, people are willing to immigrate at lower wage levels and higher housing costs, indicating that amenities are positively influencing quality of life. 

## • **High Shadow Price** 

   - A pattern of high and rising wages paired with persistently low housing prices serves as a heuristic for an amenity constraint: the location must raise wages to attract people, yet weak housing demand signals that declining amenities are undermining quality of life. Weinstein et al. (2022) operationalize this logic by estimating U.S. county quality of life from the residuals of wage and housing regressions, where high wages alongside low housing values relative to fundamentals imply low amenity-driven appeal. 

- **Changes in Changes** 

   - A city that once had strong immigration and rising housing prices, but now sees falling housing demand despite rising wages, may be experiencing a recent deterioration in amenities. 

   - If public safety worsens, public services decline, or environmental quality deteriorates, we may see a reversal in previous growth trends. 

## • **Camels and Hippos** 

- Cities with declining amenities may retain industries and residents that are less sensitive to quality-of-life factors (camels), such as legacy industrial firms or less mobile populations. On the other hand, more amenity-sensitive sectors (hippos), such as tech, tourism, and the relatively footloose creative class, may shrink or avoid the city altogether. 

## • **Getting Around the Constraint** 

- When amenities are degraded, residents may seek private substitutes (e.g., private schools, gated communities, home security) rather than relying on public goods. 

- People may relocate within the city to neighborhoods perceived as holding better amenities (a safer neighborhood, a stronger school district) 

96 

Flint, Michigan illustrates how an amenities constraint reveals itself in spatial equilibrium. Figure 13 shows that Flint has experienced above-average real income growth as compared to other US metropolitan areas, accompanied by falling housing values and net outmigration of 22,000+ residents. Real income growth was not just a reflection of a decrease in housing prices, as nominal personal income growth in the Flint MSA (4.25% annual) kept pace with the national average (4.26%) over the same period. Normally, rising incomes would be expected to attract labor, but in Flint the increase in wages was accompanied by outmigration and a collapse in home values. 

The binding constraint in Flint was the water crisis of the late 2010s, when the city’s drinking water supply was contaminated with lead following a cost-cutting change in the water source. The crisis created severe public health risks, eroded trust in local institutions, and left a long-lasting stigma attached to the city’s quality of life. This negative amenity shock undermined the city’s attractiveness to both current and prospective residents. As a result, even with growing real and nominal wages, housing values declined and population fell, indicating that people were unwilling to stay. This combination of high-shadow prices and “changes in changes” symptoms point clearly to an amenities constraint. Importantly, the rise in income indicates that the challenge was not a collapse in labor demand, but rather an inability to attract and retain labor supply despite lower housing costs and rising incomes in the wake of deteriorating amenities.

## **Summary of Differential Diagnosis** 

When testing constraints, it is important to start at the top of the diagnostic tree and work our way down. For example, we can say the quality of schools is a binding constraint for growth if we find that: 

- 1) Wages are rising and population falling (labor supply constraint), 

- 2) Housing prices are falling (amenity constraints) 

- 3) The quality of primary schools is declining, while the people are moving away from the areas with lower quality towards the areas with higher quality. 

- 4) Additionally, if schools were not always bad, we would see population decline and housing prices fall after they worsened in quality 

When testing for constraints, we should look for a combination of these tests occurring. Diagnostics work is an art, not a science. It relies on heuristics and judgment. One must interpret the signals in context, examining how they fit together and in what direction they point. It is often necessary to play around with different models or frameworks of economic growth, testing each to see which one best explains the observed patterns. This process is iterative and creative, more like assembling a mosaic than solving an equation. The data will often be imperfect, and we may not have all the information needed to perform every test. But policymakers must still make decisions, even in the face of uncertainty, by forming the most coherent diagnosis possible with the evidence at hand.

## **2.6 From Constraints to Syndrome** 

After identifying one or more binding constraints for growth in the city, the next step is to identify the underlying syndrome that explains why the constraints persist. When we identify a constraint, we are making the claim that if this constraint were relieved, then there would be a large return in terms of the city’s economic performance. This begs the question of why the city has not already solved these constraints? There would be a strong incentive to solve any challenges or constraints that directly affects people’s living standards. For example, if firms require enhanced electricity access in order to keep growing and thriving, then you might expect the chamber of commerce to lobby the city or national government to invest in electricity infrastructure. The business community and larger citizenry would support politicians pledging to do so, and the needed investment would take place and yield returns. If this does not happen, the question is why? 

A syndrome, in this context, is the underlying explanation for why a binding constraint is not automatically corrected by the normal functioning of markets or institutions. Economists are skeptical of stories that rely on people simply being inattentive or irrational. If markets were perfect, any shortage of a factor such as electricity, skilled labor, or available land would create incentives for firms, governments, or households to address the problem, and the constraint would dissolve over time. The persistence of a constraint signals that some deeper mechanism is at play, one that makes the problem reproduce itself as an equilibrium outcome. The syndrome is the analyst’s hypothesis for this mechanism: it provides a logical reasoning for why the constraint remains unresolved, despite apparent incentives to fix it. Without identifying the syndrome, it is dangerous to leap to policy prescriptions, since interventions may fail or even backfire if they do not address the root causes. 

The following are examples of syndromes commonly found in cities: 

## _**Not In My Back Yard**_ 

This political dynamic, commonly referred to as NIMBYism, can become a syndrome in a city where constraints on the supply of housing are perpetuated by local regulations because incumbents prevent the building of new housing supply with political influence or pressure. NIMBYism is often a mix of preconceptions and desires that include keeping the built environment the same or preventing greater population growth and its perceived negative consequences. Immigrants and new firms naturally do not get a vote in local policy 

99 

making, because they are not part of the city yet. Often, progress must be made by changing the politics at the city or even state and national level. 

## _**Segregation Syndrome**_ 

Another example of a political syndrome in a city is segregation or differences between groups within a city who live apart from one another. This might be overt or implicit discrimination on the part of one group towards the other. Alternatively, it might be due to the way that groups are historically living in different parts of a city. Regardless, this type of sorting can create city politics that are difficult to navigate. Growth in the city might be good on average but may help or hurt one group disproportionately over the other. If crime is an issue causing people to leave, especially the CBD, for example, then the city may want to improve safety. This is a laudable goal, but if housing is constrained in the city and in the country overall, the immediate effect of improving an amenity like safety will be to increase housing prices potentially pricing existing low-income residents. Again, this can make the politics of reform tricky. 

## _**Fiscal Spirals**_ 

This syndrome happens when fiscal revenues decline due to population decline or market crashes. Urban economic growth must be financed through public revenues which are themselves reliant on growth. City public finances are often dependent on property and sales tax revenues. Many amenities and public services in turn are reliant on strong, sustainable public finances. If cities are unable to collect adequate revenue, then they might fail to provide key public goods and services or fund amenities. A shock to a city’s export sector can start a spiral where lower economic activity leads to lower tax revenues, which leads to a decline in public services like schools and public safety, which causes further exodus, declining property values, and further revenue decline. This can cause a vicious cycle that is difficult to come out of to be able relieve the binding constraint. 

## _**Coordination Disequilibrium**_ 

This dynamic can become a syndrome when agents need to act in coordination but have no mechanism to coordinate action. Diversification implies solving chicken and egg problems. For example, the development of a biotech industry might need a strong academic or R&D ecosystem, which in turn might require funding from biotech firms. It is always risky to be a pioneer in a city, so cities may find it difficult to attract or develop industries that do not already exist in their ecosystem. Coordinating the many different actors that have to come together can be difficult without a player large enough who will internalize the positive spillovers. 

100 

## _**Policy Misalignment**_ 

A set of syndromes also stems from mismatches between the level at which policy is made and the scale of the problem it is meant to address. When policies are made at too high a level, such as at the state or national government dictating urban policy, they often fail to allow for necessary variation across cities or even neighborhoods. For example, in the United States, state-level caps on property tax assessments hinder a city’s ability to raise revenue in line with rising service demands and infrastructure needs. Additionally, state or national restrictions on extraterritorial jurisdiction constrain a city's ability to regulate land use or extend infrastructure just beyond its borders, making it more difficult to manage the expansion of the urban footprint. Even within a city, policies set at the citywide level can block neighborhood-specific approaches. For instance, mandatory parking minimums applied uniformly across a city can prevent a specific neighborhood from becoming more pedestrian-oriented, even when neighborhood residents would prefer higher density and better access to amenities over space for cars, as distinct from the city. 

## _**Administrative Fragmentation**_ 

Policy can be delivered at too low of a level, oftentimes when many municipal governments exist within a single metropolitan area. This phenomenon, known as administrative fragmentation, produces misalignment, either by fragmenting authority across jurisdictions or by narrowing the scope of who is considered in decision-making. When a single urban economy is divided across multiple jurisdictions, it will struggle to deliver public goods that require scale in delivery. The lack of coordination between districts undermines the benefits of agglomeration. For example, water utilities that serve only within a city’s legal limits may fail to extend into adjacent areas that are part of the metro’s commuting zone but lie outside the city’s governance reach. 

## _**The Politics of low-productivity places**_ 

National political incentives often override urban economic realities, leaving local growth constraints unaddressed. National or state governments may misallocate public investment toward electorally important but lower-potential places, sustaining populations in cities whose tradable base has eroded. Residents of weaker cities often have local ties and demand funds to sustain jobs and amenities. Politicians respond with transfers and public projects that keep local populations artificially high, thus winning votes but not spurring long-term growth. In many countries, nationally financed infrastructure projects are spread thin across constituencies rather than targeted to high-return corridors that could unlock agglomeration economies and serve many more people in cities. This dynamic is most visible in the misallocation of public funds, but it also applies to policy. For example, many U.S. states impose extraterritorial jurisdiction limits that prevent cities from annexing surrounding land or regulating growth just beyond their borders. These limits reflect rural 

101 

political resistance to urban expansion, yet they leave fast-growing cities unable to manage sprawl or capture tax revenue from suburban development.

## **2.6 From Growth Diagnostics to Policy Formulation** 

The growth diagnostics framework we propose is meant to help identify policy priorities that catalyze economic growth. Yet policies are always people-based, not place-based. As Glaeser puts it, “the goal of policy is to increase the choices available to people,” which means that “a policy that yields a beautiful place but does little to increase the welfare of individuals has little appeal to most economists.” (Glaeser, 2007). Resolving binding 

102 

constraints for economic growth in specific places increasing the availability of options for both firms and people. People and firms might want to move to places that excel in the provision of public goods or that allow for housing expansion. In turn, policies that keep people in low-productivity places are not really solving binding constraints but actually restricting the movement of people and firms for non-economic reasons. 

Effective economic development strategy begins with identifying binding constraints. This clarity matters because cities rarely have the luxury of addressing all their challenges at once. The fiscal, political, and administrative bandwidth of a local government is limited, and spreading effort across too many fronts dilutes impact. Policymakers face trade-offs. Addressing the binding constraint directly should be a top policy priority informing any economic development strategy, since it provides the highest bang for the buck. 

Identifying and resolving the binding constraint is not a one-time task but an ongoing process. As cities overcome one bottleneck and begin to grow, new constraints inevitably emerge. This is a natural part of the development process: more people, firms, and investment place fresh demands on infrastructure, institutions, and services. For this reason, economic strategy must be supported by systems that continuously identify and respond to the next binding constraint as it arises. This ensures that the business-enabling environment, which includes infrastructure, regulation, governance, and services, keeps pace with the demands of a growing economy. 

Working on lower-priority constraints before resolving the binding constraint can inadvertently heighten the severity of the binding constraint. For example, if transport congestion is a binding constraint to city growth, building more housing without improving transport infrastructure will increase pressure on an already strained transport system. In this case, the core constraint becomes more binding, not less. Prioritization is not just a good strategy; it is essential to avoid undermining the city’s own efforts. The failure to focus on the most binding bottleneck means policy efforts are unlikely to yield real gains, while also consuming resources that could have been used to address what matters most. 

Good local policy requires not only tackling binding constraints where growth potential is real but also recognizing that not all place-based interventions are welfare enhancing. Spatial equilibrium implies that cities are linked through migration, as people move to maximize their quality of life. When policy slows or blocks this process, it can reduce welfare by trapping residents in low-opportunity environments. Out-migration often reflects a broader rebalancing across places that no amount of local spending can reverse, as seen in cities built around a single tradeable industry that has since collapsed, such as a depleted mine. While efforts to attract new tradable sectors are worthwhile, the goal should not be to preserve the population for its own sake. Substituting private sector tradable income with public transfers or local projects may keep people in place without raising productivity. In the long run, policy should align with the forces of spatial equilibrium, not work against 

103 

them. See Box 4 on how cities that face labor shocks can adapt the urban environment to lower levels of tradable income rather than working against spatial equilibrium. 

## **Box 4: Maximizing Welfare by Aligning Place-based Policy with Spatial Equilibrium** 

The differing trajectories of the City of Detroit and the City of Pittsburgh, both hit by steep declines in labor demand, show that place-based policy is not about preserving population size for its own sake but about aligning with the forces of spatial equilibrium. For cities facing negative labor shocks, this means adapting to smaller scale by rightsizing, concentrating around core strengths, and building an urban form that matches their economic realities, rather than working against these dynamics by clinging to the past scale. 

The City of Detroit’s population fell from 1.85 million in 1950 to 639,000 today, lowering density from 13,249 to 4,608 people per square mile. This hollowing out eroded agglomeration benefits such as density of skills for complex production. Instead, Detroit was left with the challenges of maintaining oversized infrastructure to dispersed residents and struggling to provide basic public services, all while its tax base eroded. This fiscal stress culminated in the City of Detroit declaring municipal bankruptcy in 2013, the largest in United States history. Today the City struggles with a median household income of $39,575 and a 31.5 percent poverty rate (Data USA). 

The City of Pittsburgh also shrank, from 677,000 in 1950 to 303,000 in 2020, but invested in a stronger urban core. About 220,000 people live within 5 km of the city center compared to only 190,000 in the City of Detroit, despite Detroit having twice as large a population. This difference in form is striking: Pittsburgh resembles a pyramid, with a dense core tapering outward, while Detroit spreads more like a pancake, with a flat, thin population distribution that undermines central vitality. Pittsburgh’s urban form is geared towards livability, as the city rose to the top tier of American cities in the Economist Intelligence Unit’s livability index. Its Oakland district, though only 1.7 square miles, now accounts for 10 percent of city residents and nearly 30 percent of its jobs, anchored by the City’s “eds and meds” growth strategy. By refocusing on its urban core, the City of Pittsburgh reinvented itself, outpacing Detroit with a median household income of $64,137 and a 19.5 percent poverty rate. 

The contrast highlights how policy can either resist or reset in the face of negative labor shocks. The City of Pittsburgh reset by accepting shrinkage but reorganizing around a dense, vibrant core with a pyramid-like form that sustains agglomeration and urban amenities. The City of Detroit resisted, allowing sprawl to remain, density to erode, and the central city to hollow out, creating a pancake-like form that undermines productivity and livability. Cities can adapt to the losing end of spatial equilibrium by right-sizing, concentrating around core strengths, and transforming an urban built environment to match economic realities.

Although city growth diagnostics often succeed in pinpointing a binding constraint to growth, the path to policy formulation and impact is often unclear. Even when a clear constraint emerges, technical solutions often face barriers that prevent the delivery of actionable policy. Barriers to delivering the ideal technical solution can include political resistance, weak implementation capacity, or immutable structural features, such as geographic limitations. One example of a geographic implementation constraint is transport challenges in a mountainous city. There are very few, possibly no, technical solutions that will directly eliminate or resolve an identified transport constraint in a mountainous city. 

When a constraint cannot be directly resolved or eliminated, the path forward is to relax the constraint’s grip on growth by finding partial, indirect, or second-best measures. A lowdensity housing market may have identified zoning challenges as the constraint to housing growth. However, if comprehensive zoning reform of minimum lot sizes is politically infeasible, a city might begin by legalizing accessory dwelling units. These kinds of partial steps can still unlock growth even when the ideal policy solution is out of reach. 

Relaxing the constraint requires operating within the existing political and administrative boundaries, which shape the set of policies that are realistically feasible given current politics and implementation capacity. Often, the most technically sound solutions are politically unattainable or structurally constrained. Political barriers, such as entrenched interests, institutional inertia, or public resistance, are rarely resolved in the short or medium run, if at all. Meaningful progress delivered in the short or medium run is only possible by navigating within these constraints, often by advancing second-best solutions that are politically and administratively viable. 

Navigating political and administrative barriers to policy delivery requires not just up-front analysis, but active engagement. The viability of even second-best policy solutions often cannot be determined through ex-ante research. Policymakers benefit from working across stakeholders and testing actions on the ground to discover what is feasible. Stakeholder engagement surfaces practical insights and constraints that external analysts may overlook, helping shape more realistic and viable solutions. Working with stakeholders to pilot actions supports implementation bandwidth by building capacity through learning-bydoing. It also generates political support by demonstrating early wins and building stakeholder buy-in. Over time, this active engagement can expand the space for reform. Frameworks for discovery-oriented policy approaches include Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (Andrews, Pritchett and Woolcock, 2017), Adaptive Leadership (Heifetz, Linsky and Grashow, 2009), Public-Private Roundtables such as Mesas Ejecutivas (Ghezzi, 2017) and High Bandwidth Policy Making (Hausmann, 2008). 

The institutional setting for policy delivery plays a defining role in whether a city can effectively relax its constraints. In the context of urban growth, institutions must be capable of responding to the evolving needs of firms and workers across the entire metropolitan 

105 

labor market. Analysts and policymakers must ask whether the right institutional vehicles exist for effective policy delivery that match city growth challenges.  A city struggling with business attraction, for example, will require an organizational set-up that coordinates across different agencies (permitting, infrastructure, marketing, etc.) as well as distinct administrative entities to enable a functional city-wide business ecosystem. Sometimes this means creating new organizations to align with implementation needs. Other times, it involves repurposing or strengthening existing ones. 

One of the most common institutional barriers is administrative fragmentation within metropolitan areas. When a single urban labor market is divided across multiple geographic jurisdictions, key public goods such as infrastructure, land use, and transportation are delivered at too localized of a level. This fragmentation of one city into many local governments introduces jurisdictional coordination costs that undercut the benefits of agglomeration. Fragmented governance means policy delivery does not match the reality of cities as integrated labor markets. For example, water utilities that serve the legal limits of a city, rather than the entire commuting zone, will stifle housing development across an otherwise growing metropolitan area by making adjacent land non-viable for dense subdivisions. In this instance, public goods delivery is overly localized as city leadership does not have the authority to expand infrastructure to the rest of the commuting zone, which constitute separate local governments. See the Wyoming case study in Section III for details on how administrative fragmentation prohibits the delivery of public goods and regulatory coherence across a commuting zone. 

Empirical evidence confirms that administrative fragmentation undermines city growth, and that metropolitan coordination improves city growth. Ahrend, Lembcke and Schumann, (2017) finds that cities with fragmented governance structures tend to be significantly less productive. Specifically, the study estimates that a 10 percent increase in the number of local jurisdictions within a metropolitan area is associated with a 0.6 percent decline in productivity. However, the presence of a metropolitan governance body cuts this penalty roughly in half. The negative productivity impact of fragmentation is not only statistically significant, but also as large as the positive agglomeration benefits typically associated with a 10% increase in city size (Ahrend, Lembcke and Schumann, 2017). In other words, better coordination across jurisdictions can deliver gains equivalent to those of expanding the city’s population and labor market. Metropolitan governance bodies help restore these benefits by reducing interjurisdictional coordination costs and improving coherence across the urban system. As cities grow, achieving alignment in policy domains such as transport, land use regulations, and infrastructure provision across boundaries may be one of the most powerful levers for resolving constraints and supporting city growth. 

Policy formulation is, ironically, not formulaic. Policymakers must instead engage with the messy realities on the ground to build policies and systems that solve local constraints 

106 

within their specific political and administrative context. Ideally, the binding constraint can be resolved directly, but political resistance, limited implementation capacity, and structural barriers often block the path to first-best solutions. Policymakers must therefore pursue second-best measures that work within existing constraints, engaging stakeholders to surface insights, pilot ideas, build support, and adapt through action. To do this well, policy experts must act like architects—testing multiple designs and adjusting as needed during implementation (Hausmann, 2025). Solving local problems and building a prosperous city depends on practitioners who are willing to experiment, learn, and craft solutions.
