Your city is not an island

The administrative city is one piece of a larger machine: people commute, firms hire, and housing supply responds across a region far wider than the city line — the labor market. We approximate it with the metropolitan statistical area. Boston sits inside its metro area, and most of what follows is read at that scale.

Your exports matter

A city’s size tracks the size of its export sector — what it sells to people outside the region. No city makes everything it consumes (the beef, the chips, the software), so to buy from outside it has to sell outside. The export base is what a metro can ultimately support. Your City’s Most Specialized Industries

Of the tradable industries above, these are the ones Boston does an unusual amount of. Each is measured against the global benchmark: a score of 1.0 means the industry takes up the same share of jobs here as it does worldwide. Anything higher means the city is more concentrated in it than the world is — and the further past 1.0, the more distinctive it is.

Take the share of your city’s jobs that sits in an industry, then divide it by the share of jobs that same industry holds worldwide. Economists call the result revealed comparative advantage, or RCA.

If an industry is 4% of jobs in your city and 1% of jobs globally, the score is 4.0 — four times more concentrated here than in the world at large. A score of 1.0 means exactly average, and below 1.0 means the industry is thinner here than it is elsewhere.

A high score doesn’t automatically mean a big industry. It means a distinctive one — which is why the dots below are sized by how many people the industry actually employs.

How You Compare With Peer Cities

The same measure, now set against four cities of similar size and economic make-up. The tick on each row is the average of those four — where the dot sits to its right, Boston is more specialized in that industry than its peers; to the left, less. Hover any row to see the cities behind the average.

Peers are metro areas close enough to Boston in size, income and industry mix that a comparison is meaningful. Comparing against a global average would mostly tell you that Boston is a large, wealthy city — comparing against cities in the same position tells you something you can act on.

How is the metro performing?

Metro (MSA)

Plot every metro by how fast its population grew (x) against how fast its wages grew (y). Both rising is a positive demand shock; both falling, a negative one. Population rising while pay lags points to a supply shift (amenities, cheaper living); pay rising while population lags, a constrained supply. Where does Boston land?
